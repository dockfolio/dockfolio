# 04 — Content Marketing & SEO

**When to use:** When you've decided content is a distribution channel. When you're ready to commit 6-12 months before seeing traffic. Content SEO is the slowest channel to start working and the highest-compounding over time.

**Primary sources:** Tim Soulo / Ahrefs (the best practical SEO writing in existence). Nat Eliason's "Topical Authority." Julian Shapiro's growth handbook. Glen Allsopp (Detailed.com). Brian Dean / Backlinko. Ross Hudgens / Siege Media.

## The first principle: content is a moat, not a hack

$1 spent writing content in month 1 pays back for years. $1 spent on ads in month 1 is gone by month 2. Content compounds because Google's index is permanent; ads are rented attention.

BUT: content takes 6-12 months to start working. If you need customers in 30 days, content is the wrong channel. Use direct outreach (see `02-pre-traction.md`).

Content is the right channel when:
- You can commit to publishing weekly for at least 6 months
- You have a clear audience whose search intent you understand
- Your product benefits from search traffic (i.e., people Google their way to solutions — not true for every product)
- You can produce content that's genuinely better than the current top result

## The four content quality tiers

Not all content is equal. Most content published by indie SaaS is tier 4 and produces nothing.

**Tier 1: 10x content.** Original research, unique data, extensive interviews, deeply technical deep-dives. Takes 40+ hours per article. Examples: Ahrefs' own blog posts on SERP studies; Patrick McKenzie's long-form essays; First Round Review's founder interviews. Earns links, ranks quickly, drives long-term traffic.

**Tier 2: Better-than-current-top-result content.** You look at the top 5 Google results for a keyword, find what they're missing, and write the missing piece. 8-16 hours per article. Ranks if you execute. This is where most indies should live.

**Tier 3: Generic advice content.** "10 Tips for X." "How to do Y." Same information as every other result. 2-4 hours per article. Rarely ranks. Occasionally gets traffic if the keyword has low competition.

**Tier 4: AI-generated thin content.** Low-quality, high-volume. ChatGPT outputs. Google's March 2024 Helpful Content Update explicitly penalizes this. Waste of time.

**Rule:** Write tier 1 or 2. Skip tier 3. Never tier 4.

## Keyword research that's actually useful

Forget "high-volume keywords." At indie scale, you don't want high-volume keywords — you want high-intent keywords. A keyword with 50 searches/month where 20 of those are buying is better than 5000 searches/month where 5 are buying.

### The indie-SaaS keyword framework:

1. **Product-aware keywords** — "[competitor] alternative," "best [category] tool," "[specific feature] tool." These convert at 3-10%. Low volume (50-1000/mo) but high intent. THIS IS WHERE YOU START.

2. **Problem-aware keywords** — "how to [specific problem]," "[pain point] without [bad solution]." Convert at 1-3%. Medium volume. Second priority.

3. **Unaware / top-of-funnel keywords** — "what is [concept]," "[broad topic] guide." Convert at 0.1-0.5%. High volume. DO NOT START HERE. You can add these later once you're ranking for high-intent terms.

### The research process:

1. Ahrefs Free / Ubersuggest / Google Suggest: type your category and see what people search.
2. For each candidate keyword, check the current top 5 results. Can you plausibly write something better? If no, skip.
3. For each candidate, check "keyword difficulty." At indie scale, target KD < 30. Above 30 and you're competing with domain authority you don't have.
4. For each candidate, read the "people also ask" boxes. Each one is a mini-article you can write separately.
5. Make a list of 20-30 candidate keywords. Sort by (intent × feasibility). Start with the top 5.

### The "shadow keyword" tactic:

Find a keyword that's trending upward but undersupplied. Google Trends shows search interest over time. A keyword going up and to the right for 6 months with few good articles is gold. Examples from recent years: "prompt engineering" (2022), "local LLM" (2023), "MCP server" (2024-25). Writing about a rising keyword early means you rank before competition catches up.

## The content structure that ranks

Google's ranking signals are complex but the skeleton of a ranking article is boring and predictable:

1. **H1 that matches the search query almost exactly.** "How to X" query → "How to X" H1.
2. **First paragraph answers the query directly.** Don't bury the lede. Google's snippet preview comes from the first 155 chars.
3. **Table of contents (H2s).** Google uses these as jump links in the SERP.
4. **Clear sections per subtopic.** Each H2 should stand alone as a mini-answer.
5. **Original screenshots, diagrams, or data.** Stock images kill rankings post-2023.
6. **Internal links to your other content.** Creates a topical cluster.
7. **External links to credible sources.** Counter-intuitive but true: linking out to high-authority sites helps your own rankings.
8. **A clear CTA at the end.** "Want to do X without writing code? [Your product] does exactly this." Converts reader to trial.
9. **Length: 1500-3500 words for most topics.** Longer is not always better. Match the search intent. "How to delete a file" doesn't need 5000 words; "How to choose a CRM" does.

## The writing quality rule

Google's Helpful Content system now rewards:
- Content written by experts (or with E-E-A-T: Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, Trustworthiness)
- Content that demonstrates first-hand experience (screenshots from the actual product, real data, personal observations)
- Content that's NOT just a rehash of other top-5 results
- Content with a clear, identifiable author (byline, author page, LinkedIn)

It penalizes:
- Content that's clearly AI-generated without editing
- Content that just summarizes other top-10 results
- Thin content (<500 words for serious topics)
- Content farms with no author identity
- Affiliate-link-stuffed articles

Every article you publish should pass the question: **"If a random expert in this field read this, would they learn something new?"** If no, don't publish it.

## The topical authority model

Post-2023, Google rewards topical depth over individual post quality. The concept: if your site has 30 articles covering every facet of "invoicing for freelancers," Google treats you as an authority on that topic and ranks all your posts better than if you had 3 random articles.

How to build topical authority:
1. Pick ONE narrow topic your product serves.
2. Map the topic into 30-50 potential articles (subtopics, comparisons, tutorials, case studies).
3. Write the 10 most important ones first. These are "hub" posts.
4. Cross-link them aggressively (every hub links to 3-5 others).
5. Over 6-12 months, fill out the long tail of smaller articles.
6. Don't write about unrelated topics. Stay focused.

Example: If you sell invoice software for freelancers, don't write about "productivity tips for freelancers." Write about invoicing: late payment strategies, tax classification for 1099 workers, how to structure contracts, currency handling for international clients, chasing non-payers, recurring invoice setup, etc. Every article should be about invoicing, not freelancing.

## The first-year content plan for an indie SaaS

Week 1: Pick your narrow topic. Write your keyword list (30 keywords). Pick the top 5 to start.
Weeks 2-5: Write the top 5 hub articles. Publish one per week. Each should be 2000+ words, your best work.
Weeks 6-10: Write 5 more articles, this time supporting articles that link to the hubs.
Weeks 11-20: Consistent weekly publishing. Alternate between new articles and updating existing ones based on performance.
Weeks 21-26: Audit. Which articles are getting traffic? Double down on those topic clusters. Kill or rewrite the failures.

By week 26 you should have ~25 articles published. If NONE of them are getting organic traffic at that point, you picked the wrong keywords or the wrong topic. Reassess.

By month 12, you should have ~50 articles and be getting 1000-5000 organic visitors per month. If you're not, the content channel isn't working for your product, and you should reallocate effort.

## The distribution-of-content principle

Writing the article is 40% of the work. Distribution is 60%. For every article:

1. **Post it on your site** with proper SEO setup.
2. **Share to your email list** (if you have one).
3. **Post a 5-10 tweet thread summarizing it** with a link at the end.
4. **Post to LinkedIn** as a standalone article or post with link.
5. **Submit to 1-2 relevant subreddits** (with context, not just a link).
6. **Share in 1-2 Slack/Discord communities** you're active in.
7. **Reply to 3-5 Reddit/Quora/HN threads** with a link to your article where relevant.

Every article should get at least 2-3 hours of distribution work after publishing. Most indies write the article and publish. The ones who distribute get 10x the traffic.

## Backlinks: still important, mostly don't chase them

Backlinks are still a ranking signal but the tactics have changed. What works:
- **Earned links from tier-1 content.** Write something genuinely great; people link to it without being asked.
- **HARO responses.** Journalists need sources. Answer requests in your domain of expertise, get quoted with a link. Free, compounds over time.
- **Guest posts on relevant indie/niche blogs.** Not mass-scale guest posting; targeted, 1-2 per quarter.
- **Resource page outreach.** "Your post about X has a section on tools — we'd fit in that section. Here's why."

What doesn't work anymore:
- Buying links (now a penalty)
- Link exchanges with unrelated sites
- Mass guest posting on low-quality sites
- Comment spam
- PBNs (private blog networks)

For indie SaaS, skip backlink building for the first 6 months. Focus on writing tier 1 content. Links will come naturally when the content is good enough.

## AI-assisted writing: use, don't abuse

ChatGPT, Claude, and similar tools are fine for: outlining, brainstorming, rephrasing clumsy sentences, fact-checking, idea generation.

They are NOT fine for: drafting the article. AI-generated prose without heavy editing is detectable by Google and by readers. Post-March 2024, it's a ranking penalty.

The correct workflow:
1. You outline the article from your experience.
2. You write the first draft by hand.
3. You ask AI to critique the draft ("what's missing? where is this weak?") and rewrite awkward paragraphs.
4. You do a final pass in your own voice.

Time saved: 30-50%. Quality retained: high. Detectability: low.

## The technical SEO floor

A few technical things you MUST get right or content won't rank regardless of quality:

1. **Page speed.** Core Web Vitals. LCP < 2.5s, CLS < 0.1, INP < 200ms. Bad page speed = downranking. Use PageSpeed Insights to check.
2. **Mobile-first design.** Google indexes the mobile version. If mobile layout breaks, rankings suffer.
3. **Semantic HTML.** H1, H2, H3 hierarchy. Alt tags on images. Schema markup (Article, FAQ) where applicable.
4. **Clean URLs.** `/blog/positioning-for-saas` not `/blog/post?id=123`.
5. **Internal linking.** Every article should link to 3-5 others on your site.
6. **Sitemap.xml and robots.txt.** Submit sitemap to Google Search Console.
7. **HTTPS.** Mandatory.
8. **Canonical tags.** Avoid duplicate content penalties.

These are table stakes. Fix them once, then forget.

## The SEO audit loop

Monthly: look at your articles in Google Search Console. For each article:
- What keywords is it ranking for? (Sometimes different from what you targeted.)
- What's its CTR? (Low CTR = bad title/meta description.)
- What's its average position? (Positions 5-15 are "almost there"; rewrite to improve.)
- What pages have impressions but no clicks? (Rewrite the title/meta.)
- What search queries are showing your page but not in any article? (New article opportunity.)

This audit takes 1 hour per month and produces a prioritized list of "which articles to update, which new ones to write." It's the highest-leverage hour you'll spend on content.

## When content isn't working

Signs content isn't working after 6 months:
- Organic traffic is < 500/mo total
- No article ranks in Google's top 20 for its target keyword
- Traffic that does arrive doesn't convert (bounce > 85%, sign-ups < 1%)
- You're burning out on the writing cadence

Possible causes:
1. **Wrong keyword strategy.** You picked high-volume keywords you can't rank for. Rebuild keyword list with < KD 30 targets.
2. **Wrong content quality.** You're writing tier 3 content. Rewrite for tier 1-2.
3. **Wrong audience.** Your product's buyers don't actually search on Google for this problem — they hear about it from peers, or they use social/video. Reallocate.
4. **Wrong topic focus.** Too scattered across subjects. Pick ONE narrow topic and drill.
5. **No distribution.** You're writing but not sharing. Fix distribution first, content second.

Content is a 6-12 month commitment. Don't kill it before month 6. Don't keep going past month 12 if the signs are all bad.
