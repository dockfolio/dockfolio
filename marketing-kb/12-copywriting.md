# 12 — Copywriting

**When to use:** Whenever you write anything a customer will read. Headlines, buttons, landing page body, email subject lines, ad copy, onboarding text, error messages, tweet threads, launch posts. Copywriting is the sentence-level craft that multiplies everything else.

**Primary sources:** Joanna Wiebe (Copyhackers) — the best living SaaS copywriter. Eddie Shleyner (VeryGoodCopy). David Ogilvy's *Ogilvy on Advertising* (1983 but still mostly right). Joseph Sugarman's *The Adweek Copywriting Handbook*. Claude Hopkins' *Scientific Advertising* (1923, still the bible). Robert Cialdini's *Influence*.

## First principle: copy is selling, not decorating

Copy isn't about sounding smart, clever, or professional. It's about getting the reader to do one specific thing. Every sentence either moves them toward that action or wastes their attention.

**The test:** delete any sentence and ask "does removing this reduce the odds the reader takes the desired action?" If no, delete it. If yes, keep it. Most indie SaaS copy is 40% waste by this test.

## The rules of persuasive copy

### 1. Specificity beats generality

- "Saves you time" → vague, unmemorable
- "Saves you 5 hours a week on invoicing" → specific, believable

Specific numbers (5 hours, 23%, 3 steps, $47) are more trustworthy than round claims (lots of hours, significantly, a few, affordable). Round numbers sound made up.

### 2. Outcomes beat features

Feature: "Automated email reminders."
Outcome: "Never chase a late invoice again."

The feature describes what YOUR product does. The outcome describes what happens to the CUSTOMER. Customers buy outcomes.

### 3. "You" beats "we" and "I"

Bad: "We built a tool that helps businesses manage their marketing automation."
Good: "You'll stop writing the same email twenty times. Here's how."

Count the "you"s and the "we"s in your landing page. If "we" > "you," rewrite. The reader cares about themselves, not you.

### 4. Concrete beats abstract

Abstract: "Enterprise-grade security for peace of mind."
Concrete: "SOC2 Type II certified. End-to-end encryption. Your data never leaves the EU."

Abstraction is a tell that the writer doesn't actually know what they're saying. Concrete details are the evidence of expertise.

### 5. Plain beats clever

The temptation to be clever in copy is strong and almost always wrong. Cleverness adds friction ("wait, what did they mean?"). Plainness removes friction. 

- Clever: "Your competition is laughing. Are you in on the joke?"
- Plain: "See what your top 5 competitors are doing differently."

Plain wins every A/B test 90% of the time.

### 6. Simple beats sophisticated

Write at a 7th-grade reading level. Short sentences. Simple words. Active voice.

- Sophisticated: "We leverage AI-powered analytics to facilitate data-driven decision-making."
- Simple: "You'll know what's working and what isn't. No more guessing."

The reader's cognitive budget is small. Every big word spends some of it.

## Headlines: the most important copy you'll ever write

5x more people read the headline than the body copy. If the headline fails, nothing else matters. A good headline is not negotiable.

### The four job-to-be-done headline formulas

**1. Outcome + audience:**
"[Outcome] for [specific audience]"
- "Privacy-first analytics for creators"
- "Invoice software for freelance designers"
- "Scheduling for people who hate Calendly"

**2. Competitor + problem + solution:**
"[Alternative] is [specific problem]. We [solution]."
- "Google Analytics is bloated. Plausible is 1% of the code."
- "Calendly spams your clients. SavvyCal doesn't."

**3. Pain + promise:**
"Stop [specific pain]. Start [specific outcome]."
- "Stop chasing late payments. Start invoicing like a business."
- "Stop writing the same email 50 times. Start sending templates."

**4. Curiosity gap:**
"[Surprising claim] and here's how"
- "We run 30 SaaS products on $47/month of hosting — here's our stack"
- "The three-step email sequence that doubled our activation"

Avoid for indie SaaS:
- "The future of [X]" (vague, arrogant)
- "Reimagined [X]" (meaningless)
- "AI-powered [X]" (cliché, dates fast)
- "Revolutionary [X]" (unprovable, triggers skepticism)

### Headline testing

If you have any traffic at all, A/B test 3-5 headline variants. Copy is where the biggest conversion wins live; a better headline can 2-5x your landing page conversion. More impact than any other single change.

## Subheads: the supporting cast

The subhead's job: close the deal the headline opened.

- **Headline asks a question? Subhead answers it.**
- **Headline makes a claim? Subhead makes it believable.**
- **Headline is abstract? Subhead gets concrete.**

Example:
- Headline: "Invoicing that runs itself."
- Subhead: "Send invoices, chase late payments, and reconcile deposits automatically. Freelancers save 5 hours a month."

The subhead delivers the specifics the headline teased.

## Body copy that converts

### The "so what?" test

Every paragraph of body copy must survive the "so what?" test. After every claim, ask: "so what?" If the paragraph doesn't answer it, rewrite.

Example:
- "Our platform is built on cutting-edge technology." → So what?
- "Our platform processes invoices in 0.3 seconds, so your clients pay the same day." → Concrete, outcome-oriented, passes the test.

### The "show the work" technique

Instead of claiming, show. Instead of "easy to use," show a screenshot with annotations. Instead of "saves time," show a before/after. Instead of "trusted by thousands," show specific logos or counts.

Copy without evidence is noise. Copy with evidence is signal.

### Bullet points: dense value

Bullets are where you load up concrete details. Not 3 bullets — 5-8. Each bullet is ONE specific benefit, not a paragraph.

- ✅ Auto-reconciles payments with Stripe
- ✅ Chases late invoices with 3 escalating reminders
- ✅ Tracks time to invoice in under 10 seconds
- ✅ Exports to QuickBooks, Xero, FreshBooks
- ✅ Handles 20+ currencies with real-time exchange

Bullets are skimmable. Body paragraphs aren't. Use bullets for the 80% of your readers who skim.

### Active voice, present tense

Weak: "Invoices can be sent automatically by the system."
Strong: "The system sends invoices automatically."

Weak: "Reports will be generated when you click the button."
Strong: "Click to generate a report."

Active voice is shorter and clearer. Present tense feels immediate and real.

## Call-to-action (CTA) buttons

The single most important pixel on any marketing page.

### CTA copy rules

- **Verb first.** "Start free trial" not "Free trial."
- **Specific outcome.** "Generate my first invoice" > "Get started."
- **First-person works.** "Start MY trial" > "Start YOUR trial" (measured in tests).
- **No jargon.** "Sign up" > "Register account" > "Join platform."
- **No "submit."** The most lifeless word possible.
- **No "click here."** Make the button itself be the click.

### CTA copy that converts, examples

- "Start free trial" (baseline)
- "See how it works" (lower commitment, for top-of-funnel)
- "Send my first invoice" (concrete outcome)
- "Protect my site in 30 seconds" (time-specific, outcome-specific)
- "Let me try it" (permission-style, low friction)
- "Show me the demo" (specific action)

### CTA placement

- Above the fold (visible without scrolling)
- At the end of the hero section
- After the main benefits/features section
- After pricing
- At the end of the page
- Optionally: sticky header or footer for very long pages

Don't bury the CTA. Don't hide it. Don't decorate it into invisibility.

## Email copywriting (the medium with the best ROI)

See `09-email-and-lifecycle.md` for lifecycle strategy. Specific to writing:

### Subject lines

- **Short:** 30-50 characters. Long ones truncate on mobile.
- **Specific:** Tell them exactly what's inside. "Your invoice template is ready" > "Good news."
- **Personal:** "A question about [product]" beats "Company Newsletter #47."
- **Curiosity, not clickbait:** "Here's what most [audience] get wrong" not "You won't BELIEVE this."

### Email body structure

1. **Greeting.** First name. Not "Dear valued customer."
2. **Context.** One sentence connecting to them.
3. **Main content.** 3-5 short paragraphs max. One point per paragraph.
4. **Call to action.** ONE specific action with a link.
5. **P.S.** Optional but powerful. People read P.S. lines more than body copy. Use it to reinforce the CTA or add a personal note.
6. **Sign-off.** Real name, real person.

### What to cut from every email

- "I hope this email finds you well" (nobody believes you)
- "My name is [Name] and I'm the founder of [Company]" (put this in signature)
- "Thank you for being a customer" (nobody reads past this)
- "Just wanted to reach out" (filler, delete)
- "Circling back" (delete)
- "I'll keep this brief" (if you have to say it, you're lying)

### Length

- Cold outreach: 50-100 words max
- Welcome email: 150-200 words
- Lifecycle trigger email: 100-200 words
- Newsletter: 400-800 words
- Launch announcement: 200-400 words

Longer isn't better. Clearer is better.

## Value proposition statement

Every product needs ONE sentence that captures its unique value. This is the statement your whole team memorizes and uses as the north star for all other copy.

### The formula

**"[Product] helps [specific customer] [do specific thing] without [specific pain]."**

Examples:
- "Plausible helps creators measure site traffic without violating visitor privacy or slowing down their pages."
- "Calendly helps service providers schedule meetings without the email back-and-forth."
- "Buffer helps marketers post to multiple social accounts without copy-pasting the same message."

### Red flags in value prop statements

- Uses the word "solution" ("our solution helps...") — meaningless
- Starts with "We believe..." or "At [Company], we..." — about you, not them
- Mentions technology ("AI-powered," "blockchain-based") instead of outcome
- Includes more than three adjectives
- Can't be said in one breath

If your current value prop fails any of these, rewrite. Then rewrite again.

## Cold outreach copy

When writing a cold email or DM:

- **Subject:** specific, personal, intriguing. "Quick Q about [their project]" > "Collaboration opportunity."
- **Opening:** reference something specific about them. "Saw your post last week about X" or "Your article on Y made me think of this."
- **Why them:** why you're reaching out to THEM specifically (not mass-blasted).
- **The ask:** ONE specific thing. "Can I send you the link?" > "Would love to chat sometime."
- **Low-friction close:** "No worries if not" or "Just curious — no need to reply if it's not a fit."
- **Signature:** Real name, one line of credibility, optional link.

Target length: 50-100 words. 150 max.

Expected response rate for well-targeted cold outreach: 20-40% reply, 5-15% convert. If you're below 10% reply, the message or the targeting is wrong.

## Social media copy

### Twitter/X

- **Hook in first line.** Nobody reads past a boring first line.
- **One idea per tweet.** Don't cram.
- **Threads for depth.** 5-15 tweets, each with its own hook.
- **Specifics beat abstractions.** "$47/month hosting" > "cheap hosting."
- **Story beats fact.** "I killed 3 products last year. Here's what I learned" > "3 lessons from failed startups."

### LinkedIn

- **First 2 lines are "above the fold."** Only the first 2 lines show before "see more." Make them compel the click.
- **Long-form works here (unlike Twitter).** 300-600 word posts with clear structure outperform short ones.
- **Personal stories outperform marketing.** LinkedIn rewards "I" more than Twitter does.
- **Hashtags: 3-5 max.** Too many hurts reach.

### Reddit

- **Story format.** "I did X, here's what happened." Not "Check out my product."
- **Zero marketing voice.** Reddit users smell corporate copy and downvote instantly.
- **Specific and self-deprecating.** "I spent $500 on ads and got 2 signups. Here's what I learned" outperforms "How we got to $10K MRR."
- **Link in body, not title.** Title is a description; link is a detail.

## The copy review checklist

Before publishing any piece of copy, run through this:

1. **Is there ONE clear action the reader should take?** If no, rewrite.
2. **Does the headline promise something specific?** If no, rewrite.
3. **Can a 12-year-old understand it?** If no, simplify.
4. **Does it include concrete numbers or examples?** If no, add them.
5. **Does every sentence move toward the action?** If no, cut the fluff.
6. **Are there any abstract adjectives ("powerful," "innovative")?** If yes, cut them.
7. **Does the body text match what the headline promised?** If no, fix the mismatch.
8. **Would you forward this to a friend?** If no, it's not good enough.

Most indie SaaS copy fails items 1, 4, and 6. Fix those and your conversion goes up without any other change.

## Copy is written, then rewritten, then rewritten

First drafts are always bad. Professional copywriters write 5-10 drafts of the same landing page before shipping. Indie founders write 1 draft and ship. The gap is the quality.

Process:
1. **Dump draft.** Write everything you want to say, badly.
2. **Cut draft.** Remove 50%. Keep only what matters.
3. **Specific draft.** Replace every abstraction with a concrete claim.
4. **Rhythm draft.** Read aloud. Rewrite clunky sentences.
5. **Final draft.** Sleep on it. Read again. Ship.

Each pass takes 20-40% of the previous one's time. The full process for a landing page: 4-8 hours for decent quality. Skipping the process and shipping the first draft is why most indie SaaS pages don't convert.

## The "explain it to your mom" test

Read your landing page to someone outside your industry — your mom, a friend, a stranger. Ask: "What does this product do? Who is it for? Why would someone pay for it?"

If they can't answer all three after reading, the copy is broken. Rewrite until they can.

This test costs 5 minutes and catches 80% of copywriting problems. Do it before launch, before major copy updates, whenever stuck.
