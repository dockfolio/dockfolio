# 08 — Distribution Channels

**When to use:** When deciding where to spend marketing time and money. When the current channel isn't working and you're considering alternatives. When you have traction in one channel and want to add a second.

**Primary sources:** Gabriel Weinberg's *Traction* (the 19 channels framework). Julian Shapiro's growth handbook. Andrew Chen on "channel fit." Lenny Rachitsky's channel deep-dives.

## First principle: channel-product fit is real

Every product has 1-3 channels that work and 15-17 that don't. Your job is to find the ones that work and ignore the rest, not to try all of them.

Most indie founders fail by doing the opposite: they try everything at 10% effort instead of one thing at 100% effort. Channel success is not about hustle; it's about fit.

**Rule:** Commit to ONE channel at 100% effort for 8-12 weeks. If it works, scale it. If it doesn't, switch. Never run two channels at < 50% effort each.

## The 19 channels (Weinberg's list, updated)

1. **Targeting blogs** — guest posts, mentions, reviews on niche blogs
2. **Publicity (PR)** — press coverage, media mentions
3. **Unconventional PR** — stunts, viral moments, creative campaigns
4. **Search engine marketing (SEM)** — paid Google/Bing ads
5. **Social and display ads** — Facebook, Instagram, Twitter, LinkedIn ads
6. **Offline ads** — billboards, radio, print
7. **Search engine optimization (SEO)** — organic Google traffic
8. **Content marketing** — blog, videos, podcasts (compounds over time)
9. **Email marketing** — newsletter, lifecycle, outbound
10. **Engineering as marketing** — free tools, calculators, widgets
11. **Viral marketing** — loops, referrals, shareable moments
12. **Business development** — partnerships, integrations, co-marketing
13. **Sales** — direct outbound, cold call, demo, close
14. **Affiliate programs** — paying others to bring customers
15. **Existing platforms** — App Store, Chrome Web Store, GitHub, Product Hunt
16. **Trade shows** — industry conferences, booths, networking
17. **Offline events** — meetups, hackathons, workshops
18. **Speaking engagements** — podcast guesting, conference talks
19. **Community building** — owning a community of users and prospects

Plus three more since the book was written:
20. **Influencer marketing** — paid or earned creator endorsements
21. **Marketplace / directory listings** — G2, Capterra, AlternativeTo, IndieHackers directories
22. **AI-answer optimization (GEO)** — getting cited in ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity answers

## Which channels work for indie SaaS

A ranking of likely effectiveness for an indie SaaS/tool at $0-$10K MRR, based on observed success rates:

### Top tier (likely to work for most indies)

**Content marketing + SEO (combined).** Slow start (6-12 months), massive compounding. Works for any product where buyers search for solutions on Google. Cost: your time. ROI: extremely high if you commit. See `04-content-and-seo.md`.

**Direct sales / outreach.** Unscalable but works from day 1. Cold email, LinkedIn, DMs, community replies. Cost: your time. ROI: high at small scale, diminishes at scale. See `02-pre-traction.md`.

**Community participation.** Joining communities where your users already are (Reddit, Slack groups, Discord, Indie Hackers, niche forums). Slow trust-building; pays off over months. Cost: your time. ROI: very high for trust; low for volume.

**Existing platforms (launch platforms).** Product Hunt, HN, Reddit. Bursty, not ongoing. Good for credibility and first customers. Cost: your time + prep. ROI: variable but unlimited upside on a good day. See `03-launch-playbook.md`.

### Middle tier (works for some products, not all)

**Engineering as marketing (free tools).** Build a free tool adjacent to your paid product. Ahrefs Backlink Checker, HubSpot Website Grader, Zapier "connect X to Y" pages. Cost: engineering time. ROI: compounds heavily but requires a specific product fit.

**Email marketing (list building + nurture).** Newsletter, lead magnets, drip sequences. Best when paired with content marketing. Cost: time + tool ($20-50/mo for ConvertKit/Mailchimp). ROI: 3x higher than social for most B2B products. See `09-email-and-lifecycle.md`.

**Influencer marketing (micro).** Sponsoring a niche YouTuber, podcaster, or newsletter with < 50K audience. Cost: $200-2000 per sponsorship. ROI: highly variable; 10x if it fits, zero if it doesn't.

**Partnerships / integrations.** Building an integration with a bigger platform (Notion, Slack, Airtable, Zapier) and listing in their marketplace. Cost: engineering time + partnership management. ROI: high if your product pairs naturally with the partner.

**Community building (owned).** Starting your own Slack, Discord, or forum. Cost: enormous time investment. ROI: high for trust and feedback, low for direct acquisition. Only do this if you're ready to moderate daily for a year.

### Bottom tier (rarely works for indies)

**Paid ads (Google, Facebook, LinkedIn).** CAC almost always exceeds indie-scale LTV. Works only if you have strong unit economics already. Most indies burn budget here before learning. Skip until you're > $5K MRR and have data.

**PR (media coverage).** TechCrunch, The Verge, etc. Journalists want $1M+ ARR stories, not indie launches. Cost: massive time investment; ROI: near zero for pre-PMF indies.

**Affiliate programs.** Only work if you have a consistent, repeatable funnel already. If your funnel barely converts for you, it won't convert for affiliates either.

**Trade shows.** Almost never worth it for indie SaaS. Massive cost, minimal payoff.

**Offline events.** Meetups and conferences are great for networking, weak for direct acquisition. Go for the relationships, not the CAC.

## How to evaluate channel fit (the "bullseye" framework)

From Weinberg's *Traction*:

1. **Brainstorm.** List all 22 channels. Under each, write the best possible campaign you could run.
2. **Rank.** Assign each channel to one of three rings:
   - **Inner ring** (promising): 2-3 channels. These feel most likely to work for your product.
   - **Middle ring** (possible): 5-7 channels. Might work; worth considering.
   - **Outer ring** (unlikely): the rest. Dismiss.
3. **Cheap tests.** For each inner-ring channel, design a $0-100 cheap test that would give you early signal in 1-2 weeks.
4. **Run the tests.** In parallel if you can, sequentially if you can't.
5. **Double down on the winner.** The channel with the best cost-per-acquisition or cost-per-trial in the test phase gets 100% of your effort for 8-12 weeks.

The bullseye framework prevents the "try everything" trap. You're testing cheap before committing expensive.

## Channel effectiveness curves

Channels differ in their effort-to-result relationship:

**Immediate-response channels:** Direct outreach, launch days, paid ads. Effort today = results tomorrow. Good for fast validation, bad for compounding.

**Slow-compounding channels:** SEO, content, community, email list building. Effort today = results in 6-12 months. Bad for fast validation, great for compounding.

**Bursty channels:** PR, viral campaigns, launches. Effort today = results in unpredictable spikes. Good for getting noticed, bad as your only channel.

**Steady-state channels:** Paid ads (at scale), affiliate programs, integrations. Effort today = linear results. Predictable but don't compound.

**Rule:** at $0 MRR, use immediate-response channels. Around $500-5K MRR, start investing in slow-compounding channels. At $5K+ MRR, layer in steady-state channels.

Don't skip phases. Founders who try SEO from day 1 often give up before it starts working. Founders who only do direct outreach hit a ceiling because it doesn't scale.

## The "hand-to-mouth" phase vs. compound phase

Every indie goes through two phases:

**Hand-to-mouth (0-$2K MRR):** You're manually acquiring every customer. The moment you stop doing marketing, new customers stop coming. This feels precarious and it is. You need to get out of this phase as fast as possible.

**Compound phase ($2K+ MRR):** At least one channel brings customers without daily effort. You can take a week off and new customers still arrive. This is where indies become sustainable.

The transition happens when you successfully build ONE compounding channel (SEO, content, integrations, a viral loop, a community). The switch is clear: "Oh, I didn't touch marketing this week and I still got 3 signups."

Most indies never make the transition because they never fully commit to a compounding channel. They stay in hand-to-mouth forever.

## Channel-specific traps

**SEO:** Giving up at month 4 because "nothing's ranking." SEO's first fruit appears at month 6-9. If you give up early, you gave up on the right channel.

**Content:** Writing generic tier-3 content (see `04-content-and-seo.md`). Produces nothing, feels like work. Write tier-1 or don't write.

**Paid ads:** Spending without a conversion funnel that converts organically first. If 1% of organic visitors convert, 1% of paid visitors will too — and paid visitors cost more. Fix conversion first.

**Launches:** Treating PH/HN launches as THE moment. They're moments, plural. See `03-launch-playbook.md`.

**Community:** Building your own community before you have customers. Without customers, the community is empty. Join existing communities first.

**Partnerships:** Chasing big logos. Partnering with a bigger company takes 6-12 months of relationship building. Smaller, faster integrations usually return more per hour.

**Email:** Building a list without knowing what to send. List is only valuable if you have something to send to it regularly. Build the content first, then the list.

**Social media:** Tweeting into the void without a distribution strategy. Social only works with an audience, and audience building is a 12-month project, not a campaign.

## The "channel graveyard"

Signs a channel is dead for your product:
- You've tried it for 8-12 weeks at full effort
- The CAC is >3x your paid plan's first-month revenue
- You can't identify WHY the channel isn't working (it's not execution, it's fit)
- Other indie founders in your niche have tried and failed too
- Platform changes (algorithm, policy) made it harder
- Your audience doesn't actually use the platform

If 3+ of these are true, stop. Document what didn't work (so future-you doesn't retry), and move to another channel in your inner ring.

## Switching channels gracefully

When a channel isn't working, switch — but don't abandon it overnight:

1. **Document what you tried and why it didn't work.** Future-you will thank you.
2. **Archive the assets** (content, ads, scripts). You may reuse them later.
3. **Stop incremental effort** but keep the existing assets running (a blog post still ranks even if you stop writing; a Reddit account still has history).
4. **Start the new channel at 50% effort** for the first week; ramp to 100% once you've proven you can show up daily.
5. **Give the new channel a time budget.** "I'll try this for 8 weeks and then reassess."

Switching too fast is as bad as sticking too long. Give each channel a fair trial.

## Multi-channel stacking (the end state)

Successful indie SaaS at $10K+ MRR usually has 3-5 channels running in parallel:

- 1 compounding channel (SEO/content) bringing 40-60% of signups
- 1 social channel (Twitter/LinkedIn/YouTube) bringing 10-20%
- 1 launch channel (occasional PH/HN/Reddit) bringing 5-15%
- 1 partnership channel (integrations, directory listings) bringing 5-15%
- 1 outreach channel (direct sales, email) bringing 10-20%

But you don't START with 5 channels. You build them one at a time over 1-2 years. Each one is a commitment to itself before you add the next.

## The channel question sequence (for deciding what to do)

When stuck on "what channel should I try next," ask these in order:

1. **What's my current channel and is it actually working?** (Define "working" numerically.)
2. **Have I actually committed to it for 8+ weeks at 100% effort?** (Or am I just dabbling?)
3. **If yes to both, what's the current channel's ceiling?** (Is it plateauing?)
4. **If plateauing, what channels do my current customers say they'd recommend this to?** (Ask them. Their answers are gold.)
5. **Do I have a channel I've been avoiding because it scares me?** (Often the right one.)

This sequence prevents the "abandon the working thing to chase the shiny thing" trap. Most indies don't need a new channel; they need to recommit to the existing one.

## The "channel math" sanity check

For any channel you're considering, do this quick calculation:

- **Hours/week of work:** ___
- **Expected customers/month from this channel (honest guess):** ___
- **Average revenue per customer (ARPU), annual:** ___
- **Expected revenue/month:** customers × ARPU / 12
- **Implied $/hour:** revenue ÷ (hours × 4.3 weeks/mo)

If the implied $/hour is less than what you'd charge as a freelancer, the channel is probably not worth it — unless it's a compounding channel where the payoff is in year 2.

Example: 5 hrs/week on Twitter, 2 customers/month expected, $30 ARPU × 12 = $360/yr = $60/mo revenue. Implied: $60 / (5 × 4.3) = $2.80/hour. Not worth it unless you're building a long-term audience asset.
