# 15 — Product-Led Growth (PLG) Motions

**When to use:** When your product can be used meaningfully without a sales conversation. When self-serve onboarding is realistic (not a workflow-heavy, integration-dependent enterprise tool). When your unit economics work at a low ACV that can't support a human sales touch. When "talk to sales" is the wrong first step.

**Primary sources:** Wes Bush, *Product-Led Growth* (2019) — the book that defined PLG as a strategy, not just a feature. Kyle Poyar (OpenView Partners) — public PLG benchmarks and research. Elena Verna (Reforge) on self-serve funnels. Appcues and ProductLed.com for operational playbooks. Lenny Rachitsky's interviews with PLG founders.

## What PLG actually is (and isn't)

**PLG is:** a go-to-market strategy where the product is the primary vehicle for acquisition, activation, conversion, and retention. The user experiences value BEFORE they talk to a human, pay, or sometimes even sign up.

**PLG is not:** having a free trial. Having a freemium tier. Having a "try for free" button. These are tactics, not a strategy. A company can have all three and still be sales-led if the real conversions happen after a 45-minute demo call.

The test: **can a user independently reach the product's "aha moment" without any human assistance?** If yes, PLG is possible. If no, PLG is not your motion — pick sales-led or hybrid.

Wes Bush's definition: "A go-to-market strategy that relies on the product itself as the main driver of customer acquisition, expansion, and retention."

## The three PLG motions

There are exactly three viable self-serve motions. Pick ONE as primary and be ruthless about it.

### 1. Free Trial (time-bounded)

The user gets the full product (or close to it) for 7-14 days. After the trial, they're locked out until they pay or convert.

- **When it works:** products with a clear evaluation window. Users can realistically assess value in days, not weeks. Examples: Calendly, Notion teams, Linear.
- **Trial length:** 7 days for simple tools, 14 days for tools requiring workflow adoption, 30 days ONLY for tools requiring multi-person workflow approval.
- **Conversion benchmark (OpenView 2023):** median 15-25% trial-to-paid for opt-in free trial (credit card NOT required). 50-60% for opt-out (credit card required).
- **Key metric:** Time-to-Value (TTV). How fast does a new user hit the first real outcome? Under 10 minutes = excellent. Over 1 day = trial is too short.
- **Common failure:** trial is too long. 30 days of "I'll try it later" becomes 0 days of actually using it. Shorter trials force commitment.

### 2. Freemium (forever-free tier)

The user gets a limited version of the product forever. They upgrade to paid when they hit a usage limit, need a feature, or need team features.

- **When it works:** products where usage grows naturally with success (collaboration tools, storage, automations), where the free tier has genuine value but hits a clear ceiling.
- **When it fails:** when the free tier is generous enough that users never need to upgrade (Dropbox had this problem for years), or when the free tier cannibalizes paid customers.
- **Conversion benchmark:** median free-to-paid is 1-5% across all B2B freemium products. Top performers hit 8-10%. If you're below 1%, the paywall is wrong or the free tier is too generous.
- **Key metric:** time-to-paywall. How long until a typical user hits the limit that forces the upgrade decision? Too fast = users churn before they love the product. Too slow = free tier becomes the "real" product.
- **The freemium trap:** supporting a huge free user base costs real money (servers, support, emails). If free-to-paid is below 2%, the free tier is a cost center, not a funnel.

### 3. Reverse Trial (the modern hybrid)

The user starts on the full paid tier automatically. After 14 days, they're downgraded to a restricted free tier unless they pay. Popularized by Wes Bush, Gong, and Figma.

- **Why it works:** users experience the BEST version of the product first, then face the FOMO of losing features if they don't convert. This is psychologically stronger than "try to find features in the free tier."
- **Conversion benchmark:** 25-40% conversion when the free tier is genuinely restrictive. Higher than pure freemium, often comparable to free trial without the "your trial has ended" cliff.
- **When it works:** products where the premium features are obvious and high-value (analytics, integrations, team collaboration). Doesn't work when the difference between free and paid is subtle.
- **Setup:** default new accounts to "Pro trial" for 14 days, surface premium features aggressively during the trial, then smoothly downgrade with a clear "here's what you'll lose" message before the downgrade.

## The Triple-A framework: Acquisition, Activation, Adoption

Wes Bush's core model. Each stage has one job:

### Acquisition: get them in the door

The goal is NOT "lots of signups." The goal is "signups from users who match your ICP." Quality > quantity. One ICP-matching signup is worth 10 random signups.

Levers: SEO, content, word-of-mouth from existing users (referral loops), templates/tools that live outside the product (engineering-as-marketing), free tools that lead to the paid one.

Measure: **ICP-signup rate**, not raw signup count. If 90% of signups are not your ICP, you're acquiring the wrong people.

### Activation: the first "aha moment"

The single most important PLG metric. An activated user is one who has experienced the product's core value at least once.

Not: "created an account." Activation is AFTER the first meaningful outcome.

Examples:
- **Slack's activation:** "2,000 messages sent" (Butterfield's canonical metric)
- **Dropbox's activation:** "1 file uploaded to 1 device"
- **Zoom's activation:** "1 successful meeting held"
- **Calendly's activation:** "1 meeting booked via link"
- **Notion's activation:** "1 note written with 1 other user invited"

**Rule:** if you can't name your activation event in one sentence, your entire funnel is broken because you don't know what "success" looks like for a new user. Pick one. Instrument it. Obsess over it.

**Benchmark:** day-1 activation should be >40% for a healthy PLG product. Below 20% and your onboarding is broken.

### Adoption: the habit loop

Activated users become adopters when they return habitually. "Habit" means: they open the product without a prompt, more than once per week.

Adoption levers: email (not marketing email, behavioral triggers — "you haven't done X in 3 days, here's why"), in-app nudges for unused high-value features, collaboration loops that bring other users in, integrations that embed the product into their existing workflow.

**Benchmark (Reforge):** a PLG product with <25% week-4 retention is not retained. 40%+ is good. 60%+ is elite.

## The activation cheat sheet

Every PLG product needs these five things wired up BEFORE worrying about acquisition volume:

1. **Single clear CTA after signup.** Not 5 options. Not "explore the dashboard." One specific action that starts the first real use case.
2. **Empty-state screens that prefill real examples.** Nobody knows what to do with an empty dashboard. Show them. Pre-populate. Offer a "try a demo" path.
3. **Interactive onboarding, not a video.** A checklist of 3-5 micro-tasks the user completes inside the product. Each task = a step toward activation.
4. **Behavioral email sequence.** Triggered by what they DO, not by signup date. "You created a project but haven't invited anyone" → email about inviting teammates. Plain English, short, human.
5. **In-product upgrade prompts tied to value.** Don't upsell on signup. Upsell at the moment they hit the free-tier limit or need a premium feature, not before.

If any of these are missing, fix them BEFORE running ads, BEFORE doing SEO, BEFORE scaling outbound. Fixing a leaky funnel beats filling a leaky bucket faster.

## When PLG fails: the honest list

PLG is not universal. It fails when:

- **The product requires integration to show value.** If users need to connect 3 systems before seeing anything, they'll never finish the onboarding. Requires customer success, not PLG.
- **The buyer is not the user.** If the CFO buys but the analyst uses, self-serve doesn't reach the decision-maker. Needs sales.
- **Long evaluation cycles.** If a user needs 6 weeks to know if it works, a 14-day trial fails. Needs proof of concept, not trial.
- **High implementation cost.** If onboarding requires 20 hours of setup, PLG breaks. Users bail at hour 3.
- **Low purchase frequency per user.** If someone buys once a year (like tax software), there's no habit loop. Needs lifecycle marketing, not PLG.
- **High-stakes workflow.** Medical, legal, compliance tools where a mistake is catastrophic. Users want hand-holding, not self-serve.

If 2+ of these apply, PLG is probably the wrong motion. Pick hybrid (PLG for trial, sales for close) or pure sales-led.

## The PLG pricing principle: value metric > seat metric

Bad PLG pricing: "$29/user/month." This caps growth because the buyer has to justify EVERY new user.

Good PLG pricing: "$0-$X based on [usage dimension]." Where usage dimension is something that scales with the customer's success — messages sent, files stored, meetings booked, products created, emails sent, videos rendered. The customer's growth IS your revenue growth.

Examples:
- **Slack:** per-user (bad, capped) — became the growth ceiling
- **Zapier:** per task-run (good, uncapped) — grows with their automation
- **Notion:** per-user (bad) — but with AI, they moved to hybrid
- **Vercel:** per-build/per-bandwidth (good) — scales with deploys
- **Remotion/video tools:** per-minute-rendered (good) — scales with output

**Rule:** if your pricing dimension doesn't grow with the customer's success, you have a pricing ceiling. Rewrite it.

## Velocity metrics: what to track weekly

For a PLG product, four numbers matter more than any vanity metric:

1. **Signup-to-activation rate** (target: 30-50%)
2. **Activation-to-week-2-retention rate** (target: 50%+)
3. **Week-4-to-paid conversion rate** (target: 5-15% for freemium, 15-25% for free trial)
4. **Time-to-value (TTV) p50** (target: under 10 minutes for simple products)

If you're improving these week-over-week, PLG is working. If they're flat, something is broken. If they're declining, stop all other work and fix the funnel.

## PLG anti-patterns (things that look PLG but aren't)

- **"Free demo" with a mandatory Calendly link.** That's sales-led with a PLG costume.
- **"Sign up" that goes to a "we'll email you when you're approved" holding page.** Not PLG.
- **A free trial that requires a credit card, a team size, a company URL, and a phone number.** The friction alone disqualifies PLG.
- **A product demo that requires a guided walkthrough from a person.** Sales-led.
- **An onboarding flow with 15 required questions before the first feature.** Activation suicide.

If any of these are in your signup flow and you're calling yourself PLG, you're not. Fix the flow or rebrand the motion.
