# 02 — Pre-Traction (0 → 10 customers)

**When to use:** When the product is built but nobody's paying. When MRR is $0-$500. When you've tried "build it and they will come" and they didn't.

**Primary sources:** Rob Walling's *Start Small, Stay Small* and MicroConf talks. Paul Graham's "Do Things That Don't Scale" (2013). Justin Jackson's *Marketing for Developers*. Patrick McKenzie (patio11)'s early essays on Bingo Card Creator.

## The pre-traction mindset shift

You do NOT need a marketing strategy. You need 10 paying customers. Strategy is for after you have traction; before traction, you need to do unscalable things to manufacture the first signals.

The biggest mistake indies make at $0 MRR: optimizing the funnel. You can't optimize a funnel that has 3 people in it. You need more people in the top of the funnel, and at <$500 MRR, the ONLY way to get more people is to manually drag them in one by one.

## The only question that matters at $0 MRR

"Who specifically, by name, is likely to pay me for this in the next 30 days, and how do I get in front of them?"

Not "who's my target market" — that's a marketing slide. Who, by name. 10 specific humans. If you can't name them, you don't have a product; you have an unvalidated hypothesis.

How to make the list:
1. Search Reddit for people complaining about the problem you solve. Note their usernames.
2. Search Twitter for tweets asking for tools like yours. Note the handles.
3. Search Indie Hackers, Hacker News, Product Hunt comments for people in your space.
4. Ask friends if they know anyone with the problem.
5. Search Facebook Groups, Slack communities, Discord servers.
6. Look at who's already paying for your competitors (case studies, testimonials).

Goal: a list of 100 specific humans. Aim to contact 50 of them personally. Expect ~10 conversations, 3-5 trials, 1-2 paying customers. This is the baseline conversion funnel for direct outreach from cold at pre-traction; if you're below it, it's positioning or offer, not effort.

## The three things that work at $0-$500 MRR

1. **Direct outreach.** Cold email, DMs, replies to relevant posts. Unscalable but works. Aim for 30-50 personalized contacts/week. Personalization means referencing something specific about them — NOT "Hi [name]."
2. **Communities where your users already hang out.** Reddit, Slack groups, Discord, Indie Hackers, specific subreddits, Facebook groups. Join, participate genuinely for weeks, then mention your product when relevant. If you skip the "participate genuinely" step, you'll be banned in hours.
3. **Content that answers the search queries your prospects are typing.** Not "thought leadership" — specific, tactical, search-optimized content. One article per week, minimum 1500 words, targeted at a keyword with real search volume (50-500 searches/mo is plenty for indie).

Everything else — paid ads, Product Hunt launches, influencer marketing, affiliate programs, SEO at scale, press coverage — is a distraction at $0 MRR. You don't have the volume for those to matter.

## The wrong things that feel like work

- Redesigning the landing page (again). Unless the current one confuses people, stop.
- Adding features. You don't have traction; new features won't unstick this.
- Rewriting the homepage copy. Unless you've tested the current copy against 100+ visitors, you don't have data to rewrite.
- Refactoring the codebase. Whatever.
- Adding integrations nobody asked for.
- Writing documentation for a product nobody uses.
- Tweeting into the void about your "journey."

All of these FEEL productive and many are satisfying to do. None of them put paying customers into the product. If you catch yourself doing them instead of direct outreach, you're procrastinating on marketing dressed up as work.

## Cold outreach that actually works

Bad cold outreach: "Hi [Name], I built a tool that might interest you. Here's a link. Want a demo?"
Result: ignored.

Good cold outreach:
```
Subject: (something specific to them, not "Hi")

Hey [Name],

Saw your tweet last week about [specific problem]. I actually built a 
small tool that solves this exact thing because I had the same problem 
at [context]. It does [1 sentence on mechanism] and it's free for the 
first 100 users.

If you want, I can set you up with an account — no commitment, just 
curious if it solves your problem. If not, I'd love to hear why.

[Your name]
[One-line credibility statement, if any]
```

Why this works:
- **References something specific** (not a generic "saw your profile")
- **Explains WHY you're reaching out** (had the same problem)
- **Offers value first** (free trial, set them up personally)
- **Asks for something cheap** (just try it, or tell me why not)
- **Closes the loop** (promises to learn from a no)

Expected response rate on this for a well-targeted list: 20-30% reply rate, 5-10% try it, 1-3% pay within 30 days. If you're below 20% reply, the list is wrong, not the message.

## The "manually onboard the first 10 customers" rule

For your first 10 customers, you should personally onboard each one. Over a Zoom call. Walk them through the product. Watch them use it. Fix their specific problem on the call. Give them your personal phone number. Be their support team.

This is absurdly unscalable and EXACTLY what you should do. Benefits:
- You'll learn more about the product in 10 onboarding calls than in 6 months of analytics.
- They'll become your best case studies and referrers.
- You'll catch positioning mistakes in real time (watch their face when they don't understand something).
- You'll see exactly what features they use and what they ignore.
- You'll create a support relationship that prevents churn.

Customers 1-10: founder onboards personally.
Customers 11-50: founder occasionally onboards, starts to automate.
Customers 51+: mostly self-serve, founder onboards only enterprise or high-ACV.

Skipping step 1 is the #1 reason indie products die at $0 MRR with 200 signups and 0 users.

## The "charge from day one" rule

Free plans are a tax on founders with no distribution. If you have free users but no paying users, you don't learn anything about willingness to pay. Charge from day one, even if it's $5/mo. Once you have paying users, you can add a free plan as a top-of-funnel tactic — but not before.

Exceptions:
- If your product has a viral/network-effect component (Slack, Figma early days), free gets you the network. Otherwise no.
- If you're explicitly building a portfolio product with the intent to monetize later via a different mechanism (ads, SaaS tier, data), that's fine — but be honest about it.

For 95% of indie SaaS, charge from day one.

## The first $100 MRR

Your first $100 MRR is the hardest. After that, the psychology shifts: you have evidence that people will pay, you can reference real customers in marketing, you have testimonials to use. The leap from $0 to $100 is bigger than the leap from $100 to $1000.

Tactics to get to $100 MRR fast:
1. **Lifetime deals to 10 friends/peers.** $50 lifetime = $500. Not MRR but proves willingness to pay and gives you 10 users. NOT recommended as a long-term strategy but a decent first-proof mechanism.
2. **Founding member pricing.** "First 20 customers get 50% off for life." Creates urgency and rewards early adopters. Works because the early adopters are the riskiest (no reviews yet).
3. **Annual upfront.** $99/year is easier to sell than $10/month because there's no ongoing commitment anxiety. Cash flow benefits too.
4. **Direct-sell to 5 people you know.** Not "sell" as in coerce; "sell" as in "I built this, I think it would solve X for you, want to try it?"
5. **Reddit, HN, Indie Hackers launch posts.** See `03-launch-playbook.md`. Expect 1-5 paying customers from a decent launch.

## The "one channel at a time" rule

At pre-traction, you should be trying ONE distribution channel at a time, for at least 4 weeks, before evaluating. Founders who try five at once execute all five badly and can't tell which is working.

Order of preference for indie SaaS at pre-traction:
1. **Direct outreach** (highest conversion, lowest volume, zero distribution risk)
2. **Community participation** (medium conversion, medium volume, requires patience)
3. **Content for narrow keywords** (low conversion short-term, compounds over 6-12 months)
4. **Launch-day platforms** (HN, PH, Reddit, Indie Hackers — good for burst, bad for ongoing)
5. **Paid ads** (only if you have a repeatable conversion funnel, which you don't yet)

Skip steps 4 and 5 until steps 1-3 have gotten you to 10+ customers.

## Metrics at pre-traction

Do NOT build dashboards. Do NOT measure MRR growth charts weekly. You have too few data points for these to mean anything, and the vanity will eat your soul.

Measure instead:
- **Conversations per week.** Target: 10+. User interviews, cold replies, demo calls, Zoom onboardings.
- **Paying customers total.** Target: +1 per week.
- **Weekly active users (of those paying).** Target: all of them. If a paying customer isn't active, you're about to lose them.
- **Churn events.** Target: 0. Each one demands a post-mortem (call the customer).

That's it. 4 numbers. Update weekly. If you're writing about growth rates, ARR multipliers, or any metric that assumes a baseline you don't have, you're LARPing as a startup instead of building one.

## When to stop doing pre-traction tactics

You can graduate from "pre-traction" when:
- You're above $500 MRR
- You're adding customers faster than you're losing them, consistently, for 3+ months
- You have a repeatable way to get a customer (you know which channel and it works >2 times in a row)
- You have 5+ testimonials or case studies
- You have a measurable conversion rate on SOMETHING (landing page visit → trial, trial → paid, etc.)

If you don't have all five, you're still pre-traction and should be doing pre-traction tactics.

## The "don't scale yet" rule

Every "scaling" instinct at pre-traction is wrong:
- "Let me hire a marketer." No. You don't know what works yet.
- "Let me set up marketing automation." No. You have 3 customers.
- "Let me run ads." No. You don't know what converts.
- "Let me build an affiliate program." No. You don't have advocates yet.
- "Let me launch on Product Hunt." Maybe — but only if you've read `03-launch-playbook.md` and you're committing to the prep work.

Scaling is what you do when you have something that works. At pre-traction, nothing works yet. Your job is to find the one thing that does.

## The psychological trap: Twitter indie-hacker theater

Twitter indie-hacker culture glorifies the "journey": public MRR reveals, aesthetic charts, daily tweets about "the grind." This is entertainment, not marketing. For every Pieter Levels who genuinely grew his business via Twitter, there are 10,000 indie hackers tweeting into a void.

You CAN use Twitter as a distribution channel (see `08-distribution-channels.md`) but only if:
- Your audience is actually on Twitter (devs, tech PMs, designers — yes; plumbers, lawyers, teachers — mostly no)
- You're willing to commit 6+ months before seeing ROI
- You have something specific to say beyond "MRR update"

Otherwise, stay off Twitter for pre-traction marketing. It's a distraction that FEELS productive. Direct outreach is uglier but works.
