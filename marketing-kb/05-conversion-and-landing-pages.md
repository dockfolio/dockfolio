# 05 — Conversion & Landing Pages

**When to use:** When you have traffic but it doesn't convert. When bounce rate is high. When signups are low despite visit volume. When your positioning is fine but the page doesn't execute.

**Primary sources:** Peep Laja (CXL Institute), Oli Gardner (Unbounce), Joanna Wiebe (Copyhackers), Brian Massey (Conversion Sciences), Bryan Eisenberg. For SaaS specifically: Lars Lofgren, Corey Haines.

## The conversion rate reality

Most indie SaaS landing pages convert at 1-3% (visitor → signup). The best hit 5-10%. Elite pages hit 15%+ but they're rare and usually combine strong positioning with deep product-market fit.

If you're below 1%: the page is broken OR the traffic is wrong. Likely both.
If you're at 2-3%: the page is average. 6-month optimization can double it.
If you're at 5%+: the page is good. Don't touch it; focus on traffic.
If you're at 10%+: you're in rare territory. Write a post-mortem so you can repeat it.

**Before optimizing the page, check the traffic source.** If Google Ads is sending you unqualified clicks, no landing page optimization will fix that. Fix the targeting first.

## The two-second test

Load your landing page. Count to two. What did the visitor understand?

They should know:
1. What the product does (one sentence, literal)
2. Who it's for (implied or explicit)
3. Why they should care (the value, not the feature)

If they get 2 of 3 in two seconds, the page is OK. If they get 0-1, the page is broken at the hero level. Fix the hero before anything else.

## The anatomy of a converting SaaS landing page

Every section serves a purpose. Skip none, add none.

### 1. Hero section (above the fold)

- **Headline (H1):** States the specific outcome for the specific customer. NOT the feature. NOT a clever metaphor.
  - Bad: "Modern Analytics for the AI Era"
  - Good: "Privacy-first analytics your visitors can't block"
  - Bad: "The Future of Work"
  - Good: "Scheduling that stops the back-and-forth email thread"
- **Subheadline:** One sentence of supporting detail. What makes this different from the alternative.
- **Primary CTA button:** ONE button. Concrete action. "Start free trial" > "Get started" > "Learn more." Concrete always beats vague.
- **Secondary CTA (optional):** "Watch demo" or "See pricing." Never a second "Sign up" with different wording.
- **Social proof line:** "Used by 2,000+ freelancers" or "Featured on Product Hunt" or "4.9★ on G2." One line. Don't fake it.
- **Hero visual:** Product screenshot with annotations, OR a short looping video, OR an interactive demo. NOT a stock photo of a diverse team laughing at a laptop.

### 2. "How it works" or "What it does"

A 3-step explanation of the product flow with mini-screenshots or icons. Each step is a sentence. The visitor should understand, after this section, exactly what they'll do in your product.

### 3. Benefits (not features)

3-6 cards, each with:
- A bold benefit statement ("Stop chasing late payments")
- 2-3 sentences of supporting detail
- An icon or small illustration

BENEFITS, not features. "Stop chasing late payments" > "Automated reminder emails." The second is a feature; the first is the benefit. Most indie SaaS pages are 80% features and 20% benefits; invert this.

### 4. Social proof

Testimonials with:
- Full name and role (not "Jane S., Customer" — that looks fake)
- A photo (not stock; real customers)
- A specific quote about a specific outcome ("Cut our invoicing time by 80%," NOT "Great product, highly recommend")
- Ideally 3-6 on the homepage, more on a /testimonials page

Logos of customers (if you have any recognizable ones). "Trusted by Company X, Y, Z." 

Numbers if you have them: "4,200 users," "$2M in invoices processed," "500K meetings scheduled."

### 5. Feature deep-dive

Now you can list features. 4-8 features, each with:
- A clear name
- 1-2 sentence description of what it does
- A small visual (screenshot, animation, diagram)

This is where visitors who want more depth find it. Keep it scannable. Use headers and icons.

### 6. Pricing

PRICING ON THE LANDING PAGE. Not on a separate "contact sales" page. Unless you're selling to enterprise at $50K+/yr, you need visible pricing.
- 1-3 tiers (more than 3 is paralysis-inducing for most buyers)
- Clear difference between tiers (what do you gain moving up?)
- Recommended tier highlighted
- Money-back guarantee or free trial clearly stated
- Annual discount (10-20%) to drive upfront payment
- NO "contact sales" as the only option unless you're actually enterprise

### 7. FAQ

Address the 5-10 actual objections your customers have:
- "How is this different from [competitor]?"
- "Do I need to install anything?"
- "Can I cancel anytime?"
- "Is my data safe?"
- "What if it doesn't work for me?"
- "How long does setup take?"
- "Is there a free plan?"
- "Do you integrate with X?"

This is where you neutralize purchase anxiety. Every question you answer here prevents a support ticket AND converts a hesitant visitor.

### 8. Final CTA section

Repeat the primary CTA at the bottom of the page. A reader who scrolled all the way down is high-intent. Give them another chance to click.

### 9. Footer

Links to: docs, pricing, about, blog, privacy policy, terms, contact. Social links (Twitter, GitHub if open source). Trust signals (payment icons, SSL, GDPR badge if applicable).

## Common landing page killers

1. **Vague headlines.** "The modern way to work." "Productivity reimagined." Meaningless. Delete.
2. **Too many CTAs.** Visitor should be unable to miss the primary action. One primary CTA per page.
3. **Marketing jargon.** "Enterprise-grade," "synergize," "leverage," "best-in-class," "world-class." Cut every one of these.
4. **Stock photos of diverse teams laughing.** Cliché, untrustworthy. Use product screenshots instead.
5. **Hero video that autoplays with sound.** Infuriating. Mute by default if you must autoplay.
6. **Popup that blocks the content.** "Subscribe to our newsletter!" Before the visitor has read anything. No.
7. **Slow loading.** Every second over 3s kills ~7% of conversions.
8. **Mobile unfriendly.** Text too small, buttons too close, horizontal scroll. ~60% of traffic is mobile; break this and you lose most of your audience.
9. **Burying pricing.** If visitors can't find the price, they assume it's expensive and leave.
10. **"Contact sales" as the only CTA.** Unless you're enterprise only. Most indies aren't.

## The copy hierarchy

Visitors skim. Copy must work at three levels of attention:

**Level 1 (scan only, 2 seconds):** Headline + hero image. Does the visitor understand what this is?
**Level 2 (skim, 20 seconds):** All headlines, subheadlines, bolded words, CTA buttons, testimonials. Does the visitor understand the value?
**Level 3 (read, 2+ minutes):** Full body copy, feature details, FAQ. Does the visitor have their objections answered?

Write so that levels 1 and 2 alone tell the full story. Level 3 is for deep readers only.

## Writing headlines that convert

The formula most indie SaaS should use:

**"[Specific outcome] for [specific audience]"**

Examples:
- "Simple invoicing for freelance designers"
- "Privacy-first analytics for content creators"
- "Async stand-ups for distributed engineering teams"
- "Menstrual cycle tracking for people who don't want an app"

Notice: no adjectives like "powerful," "modern," "revolutionary," "AI-powered." Just the outcome and the audience.

Alternative formula for more mature products:
**"[Alternative] is [specific problem]. We [specific solution]."**
- "Google Analytics is complex and blocks 50% of visitors. Plausible is simple and blocks nothing."
- "Calendly is rigid and expensive. SavvyCal is flexible and cheap."

## The "one thing" rule

Every landing page should have ONE primary goal. Usually: get the visitor to start a free trial (or sign up, or book a demo). Everything on the page serves that ONE goal.

If your landing page also asks visitors to: join a newsletter, follow on Twitter, download a whitepaper, watch a webinar, read the blog, contact sales, AND start a trial — you have no landing page. You have a portal. Conversion will be near zero because attention is splintered.

Pick the one action. Make it unmissable. Everything else is a distraction.

## Trust signals that matter

Ordered by approximate impact on conversion:

1. **Testimonials with specific numbers** ("Saved us 20 hours/week")
2. **Named customers** (real humans with photos and titles)
3. **Customer logos** (especially recognizable ones)
4. **User count** ("2,300+ freelancers trust us")
5. **Media mentions** ("Featured in TechCrunch" — if true)
6. **Security badges** (SOC2, GDPR, HIPAA — if applicable and true)
7. **Money-back guarantee** (14 or 30 day, clearly stated)
8. **Founder story** (builds personal trust for indie products)
9. **Transparent pricing** (no "contact sales")
10. **Open source badge** (for dev tools, GitHub star count)

DO NOT fake any of these. Users sniff out fake social proof instantly and never come back.

## The CTA button

The button is the single most important pixel on the page. Details matter:

- **Color:** Contrast with the page. If the page is blue, the button should be orange/green/red. Not another shade of blue.
- **Size:** Big enough to tap on mobile without precision. 48px height minimum.
- **Placement:** Above the fold, repeated at logical intervals (end of hero, end of pricing, end of page).
- **Copy:** Verb + outcome. "Start free trial" > "Get started." "See my analytics" > "Sign up." "Generate my first invoice" > "Try it."
- **First-person works.** "Start MY free trial" outperforms "Start YOUR free trial" in most tests.
- **No "Submit"** — the most generic, lifeless button word possible.

A/B test button copy before anything else. Button copy changes alone can move conversion 10-30%.

## A/B testing discipline

You CAN A/B test landing pages, but at indie scale most tests are underpowered. Rules:

1. **Don't test until you have 200+ visitors per variant per day.** Below that, statistical significance is impossible and you'll draw false conclusions.
2. **Test ONE element at a time.** Button copy. Headline. Hero image. Not all three simultaneously.
3. **Run tests for at least 1 full week.** Day-of-week effects are real.
4. **Require 95% significance.** Below that, you're just noise-chasing.
5. **Start with the biggest elements.** Headline > hero image > CTA copy > button color > body copy. Don't start with button color.

If you have <1000 visitors/week, don't A/B test at all. Just make your best guess, ship, and measure overall changes month-over-month.

## The single-page landing page vs. multi-page site

For indie SaaS under $100K ARR, a single long landing page usually outperforms a multi-page site because:
- All information is in one place; visitors don't have to navigate
- Scroll-depth analytics tell you where people drop off
- Fewer pages to design, maintain, and keep consistent
- Mobile users prefer scrolling to navigating

Multi-page makes sense when:
- You have multiple distinct products and each needs its own page
- Your sales cycle is long and visitors return multiple times
- You're doing serious content marketing and need a blog section
- You have enterprise customers who expect "About Us," "Careers," "Press," etc.

Default to single-page until you outgrow it.

## Analytics to measure conversion

Minimum required on every landing page:

1. **Plausible / Fathom / GA4** — page views, unique visitors, bounce rate, top referrers
2. **Microsoft Clarity (free)** — heatmaps, session recordings, scroll depth. The session recordings alone are worth it; you'll learn more in 10 recordings than in 100 hours of speculation.
3. **Conversion event tracking** — signup button clicks, trial starts, paid upgrades
4. **Source attribution** — which channel brought the visitor (UTM params, referrer)

Don't over-instrument. These four tools answer 95% of landing page questions. Every additional tool is a complexity tax.

## When to rebuild vs. iterate

Rebuild the page from scratch when:
- Conversion is < 0.5% (it's broken, tweaking won't fix)
- Positioning has changed (page is selling the wrong thing)
- The product has changed significantly
- You've been iterating for 6+ months with diminishing returns

Iterate the existing page when:
- Conversion is 1-4% (just needs optimization)
- You have data showing specific sections under-perform
- You're testing specific hypotheses (headline variants, pricing tiers)

Rebuilding feels productive but is often procrastination. Iteration is harder and usually better.

## The "show, don't tell" rule

Every claim needs evidence. If you say "easy to use," show a screenshot. If you say "saves time," show the before/after. If you say "beloved by customers," show specific testimonials. Claims without evidence are noise.

"Powerful analytics" → [screenshot of the actual dashboard].
"Built for teams" → [animation showing multi-user collaboration].
"Privacy-first" → [illustration of what data you collect vs. what you don't].

Indie SaaS landing pages that outperform their category are almost always heavy on product screenshots and light on adjectives.
