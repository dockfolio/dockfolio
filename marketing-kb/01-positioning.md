# 01 — Positioning

**When to use:** Before writing ANY marketing copy. Before launch. Before picking distribution channels. Whenever growth is stuck and you can't explain why. Positioning is upstream of everything.

**Primary source:** April Dunford's *Obviously Awesome* (2019). If you only read one marketing book, read this one. Secondary: Al Ries & Jack Trout's *Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind* (1981) — old but the fundamentals are still right.

## What positioning actually is

Positioning is the answer to: **"How does your customer decide what category you belong to and why you're the best option in that category for them?"**

Three things, in order:
1. **The context they put you in** (your category). Customers can't evaluate products without a frame of reference. If they don't know what category you're in, they'll pick one for you — and usually the wrong one.
2. **The alternatives they compare you to.** Never just "other tools like this." Often the alternative is a spreadsheet, a manual process, or doing nothing.
3. **The unique value you deliver that the alternatives can't.** Not features — outcomes that only you can deliver.

If you can't answer these three questions in 30 seconds, your positioning is broken, and every dollar spent on marketing is wasted.

## The April Dunford framework (distilled)

1. **List your competitive alternatives.** What do customers use today when they don't use you? Don't skip this — most founders list their direct competitors and miss the actual alternatives (spreadsheets, manual work, hiring someone).
2. **List your unique features/capabilities.** Not what you do — what you do that competitors can't.
3. **Translate features into unique VALUE.** If you have a "real-time collaborative editor," the value is "your team stops emailing version-3-final-FINAL.docx around." The feature is the mechanism; the value is the outcome.
4. **Identify customers who care about that value.** Not all customers — the ones for whom this value is urgent, expensive, or both. These are your "best-fit customers."
5. **Find a market frame that makes your value obvious.** Your category is a choice, not a fact. The same product can be positioned as "spreadsheet replacement" or "database for non-technical teams" or "no-code app builder" — and which frame you pick dramatically changes who buys and what they pay.
6. **Layer on a trend if it helps.** Trends create urgency. "Remote work" in 2020, "AI" in 2023-2025, "privacy" after GDPR. Be careful — trends also date fast.

## The worst positioning mistakes

1. **Positioning as a feature checklist.** "We have X, Y, Z, and A, B, C." Customers don't buy feature lists. They buy outcomes.
2. **Positioning against the wrong competitor.** If you position against "Notion" but your real competitor is "spreadsheets + Slack," you're in a war you can't win against a market you don't need.
3. **Refusing to narrow.** "Our product is for startups AND enterprises AND freelancers AND teachers." No. Pick one. You can broaden later; you can't narrow later without killing the product.
4. **Positioning by analogy.** "We're the Uber for X." This worked from 2012-2015 and has been tired for a decade. It also signals you haven't thought through what's unique.
5. **Tagline as positioning.** A tagline is a symptom of positioning, not positioning itself. "Software that builds itself" is a clever line. It's not positioning.
6. **Positioning by technology.** "AI-powered [X]" tells customers about your technology, not their outcome. Nobody buys AI; they buy what AI does for them.

## Positioning for indie SaaS: the narrow-and-specific rule

When you're a solo founder with no marketing budget and 3 hours a day, **the narrower and more specific your positioning, the better**. Counter-intuitive but mathematically obvious: with a small market, a 10% capture of a narrow niche beats a 0.01% capture of a broad market.

Examples of narrow positioning that worked:
- **Fathom Analytics:** "Privacy-first website analytics for people who want Google Analytics replaced, not extended." Not "analytics for everyone."
- **Savvy Cal:** "Scheduling for people who hate Calendly's spam." Not "calendar software."
- **Ghost:** "Publishing for independent writers who don't want WordPress." Not "CMS for everyone."
- **Plausible:** "Simple, lightweight, GDPR-compliant Google Analytics alternative." Narrow compared to "analytics platform." Won them $1M+ ARR.

Observation: every one of these products could have been positioned broader, and every one of them deliberately chose narrower. The narrowness is the moat.

## The 10-second test

Ask five people outside your bubble: "After reading the homepage, what does this product do and who is it for?" If any of them can't answer in one sentence, your positioning is broken.

Don't ask them "do you understand it?" — they'll say yes to be polite. Ask them to tell you what it is.

## How to test positioning cheaply

1. **The Google Ads headline test.** Run $20 of Google Ads with 3-5 different headlines targeting the same search term. Whichever gets the highest CTR tells you which positioning resonates. You don't even need the product to convert; CTR alone is the signal.
2. **The landing-page-only test.** Build a landing page with one positioning angle. Drive 500 visitors to it. Measure: bounce rate, signup rate, time on page. Change the angle, repeat. Pick the winner.
3. **The cold outreach test.** Send 50 cold emails with one positioning angle and 50 with another. Measure reply rate. Whichever gets the most replies is the winning angle.
4. **The Reddit comment test.** Free. Post an answer to a relevant Reddit thread using one positioning angle. Measure upvotes and the ratio of curious replies to dismissive ones.

All four cost less than $100 combined and produce better positioning signal than any workshop or brainstorm.

## The relationship between positioning and pricing

Positioning and pricing are joined at the hip. If you position as "the cheap alternative," you can't charge premium. If you position as "the premium option," you can't price at $5/mo without confusing customers.

**Rule:** Pricing is a positioning signal. Customers read the price and form an opinion about the product before using it. A $99/mo product is assumed to be more "serious" than a $9/mo product even if the $9/mo product is objectively better. Don't underprice if you want to be taken seriously.

## The relationship between positioning and product

Positioning constrains what features you build. A positioning of "the simplest todo app for people who hate notion" forbids you from adding Notion's features — doing so destroys the positioning. This is a feature of the framework, not a bug: good positioning gives you a principled reason to say no.

If you can't say no to features because you don't have positioning, you'll build a bloated product nobody wants. Positioning is your feature roadmap's compass.

## Repositioning

You CAN reposition, but it's expensive and slow. Signs you need to:
- Your best customers are describing you in a way you didn't intend
- You're in a price war with competitors and losing
- Your category has shifted underneath you (e.g. "Slack killers" became "async tools" in 2022)
- A bigger player entered your category and you're losing on features you can't match

How to do it:
1. **Internally, first.** Rewrite your positioning statement. Get the whole team aligned.
2. **Landing page next.** Rewrite hero, subhead, features section.
3. **Outbound messaging.** Sales scripts, ads, cold emails.
4. **Product changes.** Rename features if needed to match the new frame.
5. **Tell existing customers.** They'll be confused; explain the shift and why it helps them.

Expect 3-6 months for a repositioning to "land" in the market. Don't give up in month 2.

## Positioning for a portfolio (this is specific to Dockfolio's situation)

When you have 20+ products, you can't treat each one's positioning as independent. They're a portfolio, and the portfolio has its own positioning. Two patterns:

1. **The shared-operator-story portfolio.** "All products built by one indie engineer, all focused on privacy/simplicity/etc." Products inherit the operator's positioning (see: pieter.com/Nomad List/Remote OK).
2. **The independent-brands portfolio.** Each product stands alone with its own positioning, and the common operator is invisible. Products compete in different categories with no shared story.

Option 1 is cheaper (one story to tell) but caps each product's ceiling (it can only go as big as the operator's brand). Option 2 is more expensive (N stories to tell) but each product can scale to its true market size.

For indie portfolios with <$10K MRR total, **Option 1 is almost always right** because the operator's story is the only story that has any existing audience at all.

## Quick reference: the positioning statement

Write this one sentence. Iterate until it's tight. Use it as the north star for every piece of marketing copy:

**"[Product] is a [category] for [specific customer] who [specific job-to-be-done], unlike [main alternative] which [limitation]. We [unique mechanism] so you [specific outcome]."**

Example: "Plausible is a web analytics tool for independent site owners who want simple numbers without tracking visitors, unlike Google Analytics which is complex and privacy-hostile. We skip cookies and fingerprinting so you stay GDPR-compliant without cookie banners."

Every indie SaaS should have this sentence memorized and printed on the wall.
