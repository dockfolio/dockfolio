# 11 — Kill Criteria & Pivots

**When to use:** When you're not sure if a product should continue. When you've been building for 6-18 months without clear traction. When you're thinking about a pivot. When you're trying to decide between persistence and delusion.

**Primary sources:** Annie Duke's *Quit: The Power of Knowing When to Walk Away*. Eric Ries on pivots (*The Lean Startup*). Rob Walling on when to kill SaaS. David Heinemeier Hansson on stubbornness vs. delusion. Paul Graham's "Do Things That Don't Scale" vs. "Be Relentlessly Resourceful."

## The uncomfortable first principle

**Most indie SaaS should be killed before they are, not after.** The psychology of founders is biased toward persistence ("just one more feature, one more launch, one more month") because quitting feels like failure. But persistence in a losing product is more expensive than quitting, because it has opportunity costs: time you could be building something that works.

**Sunk cost fallacy is the #1 killer of indie founders.** Every hour spent on a zombie product is an hour not spent on a product with a pulse.

## The kill criteria framework: write them BEFORE you start

Annie Duke's core insight: **you should define the conditions under which you'd quit BEFORE you're emotionally invested.** Because once you're invested, you'll rationalize continuing no matter what the evidence shows.

Before (or at least early in) building a product, write down:

1. **What evidence would tell me this isn't working?**
2. **What's the deadline by which that evidence should be clear?**
3. **What's the threshold below which I'll kill, pivot, or sunset?**

Example kill criteria for a new indie SaaS:

- **Month 3:** If I don't have 5 paying customers, something is wrong. I'll pivot positioning or channel.
- **Month 6:** If I don't have 20 paying customers, something is wrong. I'll rethink the product or switch audience.
- **Month 12:** If I'm below $500 MRR, I'll seriously consider killing it.
- **Month 18:** If I'm below $2K MRR, I will kill it.

These are YOUR numbers; adjust based on your situation. But WRITE THEM DOWN. When you hit them, you're committed to the decision in advance.

## The "good signs" vs. "bad signs" list

While you're building, maintain two running lists:

**Good signs (these suggest you should keep going):**
- Weekly new paying customers, even if slow
- Customers spontaneously referring friends
- Customers complaining when you remove features (means they care)
- Customers asking for things you're about to build
- Users emailing you unprompted to say thanks
- 40%+ of active users say they'd be "very disappointed" if it disappeared (Sean Ellis test)
- Retention curve flattens instead of hitting zero
- One clear distribution channel producing signups
- Unsolicited press/social mentions
- Someone trying to pay you more (expansion revenue signal)

**Bad signs (these suggest you should kill or pivot):**
- Signups followed by rapid churn
- Crickets when you email your list
- "I don't understand what this does" from multiple users
- Nobody refers, nobody shares
- You're writing content and getting zero traffic after 6+ months
- Conversion rate stuck below 1% despite iteration
- You hate working on it
- Every new customer requires 10+ hours of manual acquisition
- Existing customers churn faster than new ones arrive
- Revenue chart is a flat line or going down

**The ratio matters.** 0 good signs and 10 bad signs = kill. 5 good signs and 3 bad signs = keep going, address the bad ones. If you can't honestly list 3+ good signs after 6 months, the product is probably dying.

## The three options: kill, pivot, persist

Once you've hit your kill criteria (or accumulated 5+ bad signs), your options are:

### 1. Kill (sunset the product)

When: the problem you're solving isn't real, the market doesn't exist, or you genuinely don't want to keep working on it.

How to kill gracefully:
- **Notify existing customers** 60-90 days in advance.
- **Stop charging** new customers immediately; cancel auto-renewal for existing.
- **Offer a data export** so they can leave with what they created.
- **Recommend an alternative** if you can; builds goodwill.
- **Publish a post-mortem** — what worked, what didn't, what you learned. This is career-valuable for you AND valuable to the community.
- **Archive, don't delete.** Keep the repo, domain, and data for a year in case someone wants to buy it or you want to revive.
- **Don't feel bad.** Most indie SaaS die. Killing is a normal, healthy part of the process.

### 2. Pivot (change something significant)

When: there's real signal somewhere, but not where you're pointing. Maybe one feature has users and the rest don't. Maybe one audience loves it and others don't. Maybe the pricing is off by 10x.

Types of pivots:
- **Audience pivot:** same product, different customer. "I built this for marketers but the people using it are all product managers." Rebrand to target PMs.
- **Feature pivot:** one feature is the actual product. "The email analyzer was a side feature, but everyone uses it more than the main thing." Cut everything else; focus on the email analyzer.
- **Positioning pivot:** same product, same customer, different messaging. "We were positioned as 'simple analytics' but customers buy us as 'GDPR-compliant analytics.'" Change the homepage, stop fighting the market's interpretation.
- **Channel pivot:** same product, different distribution. "Content marketing didn't work but direct sales does." Stop blogging, start outreach.
- **Pricing pivot:** same product, different pricing model. "$9/mo isn't working — maybe this should be $99/mo as a professional tool, or $0 as a free tool with a services upsell."
- **Business model pivot:** same product, different revenue source. "The SaaS isn't paying — but maybe I can license the code, or run the service for customers, or open source it with a paid hosted tier."

**Rule:** change ONE thing at a time in a pivot. Changing everything at once means you can't tell what worked.

### 3. Persist (keep going with current strategy)

When: you have good signs, the bad signs are explainable, and you haven't given the current strategy enough time.

Valid reasons to persist:
- You're within your pre-defined timeline and haven't hit kill criteria yet
- The bad signs are external (market downturn, temporary platform issue) and likely to pass
- You've identified a specific fixable problem and haven't tried the fix yet
- Customers love what exists; you just need to find more of them
- You have clear evidence of compounding (SEO growing, audience growing, MRR growing)

Invalid reasons to persist:
- "I've already invested so much time" (sunk cost)
- "I'll feel like a failure" (ego)
- "One more launch will work" (hope without evidence)
- "My friend's product took 3 years to work" (survivorship bias)
- "I can't think of what else to build" (fear of uncertainty)

If you're persisting for an invalid reason, you're in denial. Kill or pivot.

## The "six good months" rule

When debating kill vs. pivot vs. persist, ask: **"In the last 6 months, has this product gotten meaningfully better on the metrics I care about?"**

- Meaningful = 2x or 3x improvement in a key metric, not 10% wiggle
- 6 months = enough time to see through noise, short enough to not waste a year

If yes, persist. If no, pivot or kill.

6 months of flat metrics isn't "stable." It's a product failing to grow. Stability at $200 MRR is just a slow death.

## The "founder energy" metric (soft but critical)

Ask yourself weekly: **"Am I excited to work on this product today?"**

Not every day — you'll have bad days on any project. But if the answer is NO for 4+ weeks straight, that's a real signal. Founders don't build successful indie products they hate. The energy burn is too high.

This isn't just self-care. It's decision-relevant. Indie products require outsized personal commitment. If you hate it, you won't do the hard things needed to make it work, and you'll rationalize the avoidance as strategy.

If the founder energy is dead, kill or pivot — don't try to white-knuckle through.

## The "what else could I be building" exercise

Every month, spend 30 minutes asking: **"If I weren't working on this, what would I build instead?"**

Make a list of 3-5 alternative product ideas. For each:
- Is it more exciting than the current one?
- Does it address a problem you understand deeply?
- Is the market more obvious?
- Would you be better positioned to build it?

If one of the alternatives is clearly more compelling than your current product — and not just "grass is greener" optimism — that's a signal you should consider pivoting TO that alternative or killing the current one to start fresh.

If nothing beats the current product, persist with confidence.

## The "opportunity cost" calculation

How to think about opportunity cost in dollars:

- Your time is worth ~$50-100/hour on the market (freelance rate for an experienced builder)
- You spend 20 hours/week on the product
- Monthly time cost: 20 × 4.3 × $75 = **$6,450/mo in opportunity cost**
- If the product is making < $500 MRR after 12 months, you've spent ~$77K in opportunity cost for $500/mo in revenue

This math is brutal but important. Any indie product that hasn't generated more revenue than its opportunity cost after 12-18 months is an expensive hobby, not a business.

Decision rule: if (monthly opportunity cost × months invested) > (product lifetime revenue to date) × 2, you're losing money. Kill or pivot.

## The "product has real users, but not enough" dilemma

Common situation: 30-100 paying customers, $500-2K MRR, has been growing slowly for 12 months, hasn't died but isn't growing fast.

Options:
1. **Keep it in maintenance mode.** Spend 5 hours/week maintaining instead of 40. Use the remaining 35 hours/week on a new product. Treat the maintained product as a small passive income source.
2. **Sell it.** Microacquire, Acquire.com, Flippa, direct sale. Small SaaS products sell for 2-4x annual revenue. A $1K MRR product = $24-48K. Not retirement money but clean exit capital for the next project.
3. **Open source it.** Kill the commercial version but release the code. Doesn't make money but preserves the effort and creates reputation capital.
4. **Persist harder.** If you have evidence a specific strategy would 10x the growth, commit to it for 3 months.

**Maintenance mode is valid and underused.** Indies treat it as failure ("I couldn't make it grow!") when it's actually a reasonable end state for a product that works for its users but isn't venture-scale.

## Pivoting gracefully: the "pre-mortem" exercise

Before committing to a pivot, do a pre-mortem:

1. **Imagine you're 6 months into the pivot and it failed. What went wrong?**
2. **Write 5 specific reasons it failed.**
3. **For each reason, what would have been the early warning sign?**
4. **Are any of those early warning signs already present?**

If yes to the last question, your pivot is probably wrong. Reconsider.

Pre-mortems catch wishful thinking that pitch decks can't. They force you to imagine the failure mode concretely.

## The "kill one, keep the rest" portfolio decision

If you're running multiple products (like the Dockfolio portfolio case), kill criteria get weirder. One product draining time from the others is a reason to kill even if it's profitable.

Portfolio kill criteria:
- **Time cost.** How many hours/week does this product need to stay alive? If it's > 20% of your weekly capacity, it's too expensive relative to its revenue.
- **Cognitive load.** Does this product take up mental space even when you're not actively working on it? (Support tickets, outages, complaints.) High cognitive load = kill candidate.
- **Strategic fit.** Does this product reinforce your other products (shared audience, shared tech, shared distribution)? Or is it a one-off that distracts?
- **Revenue per hour.** Calculate each product's revenue per hour of founder time. Kill the bottom 20% of the portfolio annually.

Portfolio maintenance is a job; the job is to be ruthless about which products earn their keep and which don't.

## The "we built the wrong thing" epiphany

Sometimes in month 6-12 you realize: the product is fine, the market is fine, but you built the wrong product for the market. Example: built a $99/mo enterprise tool when the market wanted a $9 consumer tool. Or built a self-serve SaaS when the market wanted done-for-you services.

This isn't a pivot in the usual sense. It's more like "restart with the same domain knowledge but different execution." Signs:
- Customers keep asking for things that would be a different product
- Your positioning works for ONE customer type but 80% of inbound is a different type
- Your competitors are growing with a different model and you're not
- You have the insights but executed them wrong

Decision: kill the current product, start fresh with the learnings. This is painful but often faster than trying to mutate the existing product into the right shape.

## The "let it run" option (for products you can't decide about)

If you can't decide whether to kill or persist, one option is: **stop actively working on it, but let it run.**

- Don't promote it
- Don't add features
- Handle only critical bugs and support
- Revisit in 3-6 months to see if anything surprising happened
- If it grew on its own, great — restart effort
- If it flatlined or declined, kill with a clear conscience

"Let it run" is a valid decision when you're genuinely uncertain. It costs near-zero and produces data. The only trap is letting it run forever without ever actually making the decision.

## The final test: "would I build this again, knowing what I know now?"

If you could go back 12 months with your current knowledge, would you start this product? If YES, persist. If NO, kill or pivot.

This is the cleanest question because it strips away sunk cost. You're not deciding whether to waste the past; you're deciding whether you'd CHOOSE this again from scratch. Most dying indie products fail this test cleanly.

## A final word: killing is not failure

Indie culture celebrates persistence to a fault. "Never give up" is the dominant narrative. The reality is more nuanced: the best indie founders kill products all the time. Pieter Levels has killed dozens. Every Tinyseed or MicroConf veteran has a kill list.

**Killing a dying product is a skill, not a failure.** It's the skill that lets you redirect your finite time toward products that have a chance. Founders who can't kill get stuck on zombies for years; founders who can kill compound their learning across multiple attempts until one works.

Write your kill criteria now, before you need them. When the time comes, follow them.
