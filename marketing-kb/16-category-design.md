# 16 — Category Design

**When to use:** Only when you're stuck in a crowded existing category and losing on features you can't match. Only when you have a genuinely different point of view about how a problem should be solved. Only when you're willing to spend 12-24 months evangelizing a new frame instead of selling a product. For 90% of indie SaaS, **you should NOT do category design** — just compete in an existing category. Read this file to understand when it's the right move and (more importantly) when it's a trap.

**Primary sources:** Al Ramadan, Dave Peterson, Christopher Lochhead, Kevin Maney, *Play Bigger: How Pirates, Dreamers, and Innovators Create and Dominate Markets* (2016). Geoffrey Moore, *Crossing the Chasm* (1991, updated 2014) — the canonical text on early adopter → mainstream transition. Andy Raskin on strategic narrative. April Dunford on when NOT to design a category (see `01-positioning.md` for why narrow positioning usually beats category creation for indies).

## The uncomfortable truth about category design

**Category kings win 76% of the economic value in their category.** (Play Bigger, analyzing top-10 tech categories 2000-2015.) Second place gets 15%. Everyone else fights over 9%. This is why "being different" matters more than "being better" — different creates a new category, better just splits the existing one.

But: creating a new category is INSANELY EXPENSIVE. It requires convincing the market that the OLD way is broken and the NEW way is the right frame. This is an 18-month evangelism campaign, not a marketing push. Salesforce spent years telling people "no software" before "SaaS" was a category. HubSpot spent years defining "inbound marketing." Drift spent years defining "conversational marketing."

For a solo founder with $0 MRR and 3 hours a day: **almost always the wrong move**. For a VC-backed company with 18 months of runway: sometimes the right move. Know which you are.

## The three phases of category creation

Play Bigger's framework:

### Phase 1: Category design (6-12 months, pre-launch)

- **Name the problem nobody else is naming.** This is the hardest part. If a problem already has a name ("email marketing automation"), you're fighting an existing category. You need a problem the market feels but hasn't articulated. Example: Drift named "buyers hate filling out forms" → created "conversational marketing."
- **Name the category.** Not your product name — the category name. It should be 2-3 words, evocative, and describe the solution concept. "Software as a Service." "Marketing automation." "Conversational marketing." "Customer data platform." The name should suggest the POV ("the old way is broken, this is the new way").
- **Write the POV document.** 2-3 pages. The structure: (1) the problem, (2) why the old way can't solve it, (3) the forcing function (why now), (4) the new category, (5) the principles of the new category. This document becomes the manifesto. Everything else (product, marketing, sales) aligns to it.

### Phase 2: Market education (12-24 months, from launch)

- **Evangelize the problem before the product.** Blog posts, conference talks, podcasts, tweets. Every piece of content frames the PROBLEM first and sneaks the product in as one solution. 80% problem, 20% product. This is exhausting and counterintuitive.
- **Enlist early adopters who co-author the category with you.** The first 10 customers aren't buyers — they're co-conspirators. Include their stories in the category narrative. "At [customer], they stopped using [old category] because [new category principle]." Their voices legitimize the new frame.
- **Win one industry analyst or thought leader.** If Gartner names your category, or if Lenny Rachitsky writes about it, or if Ben Thompson on Stratechery covers it, the category becomes real in the industry's eyes. This is worth more than $1M in ads.
- **Avoid the temptation to describe yourself in the old category's terms.** The moment you say "we're like [existing category] but better," you've lost the category design game and become a feature of the existing category. Never do this.

### Phase 3: Category domination (24+ months, post-PMF)

- **Become synonymous with the category.** When people say the category name, they should think of you first. This is the end state of category design — you and the category are inseparable.
- **Raise the moat with ecosystem moves.** Partner programs, APIs, certifications, conferences. These create switching costs that competitors can't replicate with just a better product.
- **Defend the category narrative.** When a competitor tries to muddy the category definition, push back publicly. The category's health is more important than any individual feature war.

Most companies don't make it past Phase 1. Most that make it past Phase 1 don't make it past Phase 2. This is why category design is rare.

## Crossing the Chasm: the early-adopter → pragmatist gap

Geoffrey Moore's canonical insight: the Technology Adoption Lifecycle (innovators → early adopters → early majority → late majority → laggards) has a HUGE GAP between early adopters and the early majority, which he calls "the chasm."

- **Early adopters** buy because the product is NEW and DIFFERENT. They tolerate bugs. They pay full price. They evangelize. They are visionary weirdos, and they are 5-15% of the market.
- **Early majority (pragmatists)** buy because the product is PROVEN. They want references. They want case studies. They want integration with their existing tools. They want a complete product, not a visionary prototype. They are the "real market," and they are 30-35% of the market.

**The chasm:** what early adopters love, pragmatists hate. Visionary messaging repels pragmatists. Pragmatist messaging bores early adopters. If you try to sell to both at once, you fail at both.

**Moore's solution: the bowling alley.** Pick ONE pragmatist segment (a specific vertical, a specific company size) and become 100% dominant in that niche. Use that dominance as a reference to cross into the next segment. Don't try to sell to everyone at once — sell to ONE tightly-defined group until you own it, then leverage.

Example: Salesforce early on focused on "SaaS CRM for small-to-medium sales teams replacing SFA tools." Not "CRM." Not "sales software." A specific bowling pin. Once they owned it, they expanded.

## When to create a category vs. compete in one

**Create a category when:**
- You have a genuinely different POV about the problem space (not just a better feature set)
- You can articulate the "old way is broken" story in 30 seconds and people nod
- You have 18+ months of runway to evangelize before revenue scales
- There's no existing category that captures what you do (genuine white space)
- You're willing to pay the cost of education (content, talks, community)

**Compete in an existing category when:**
- You're an indie with $0 MRR and 3 hours a day
- Buyers already know what they want and you just need to show them you're a better option
- The existing category has clear buyer expectations (pricing, features, onboarding patterns)
- You can win on narrow positioning within the category (see `01-positioning.md`)
- You don't have 18 months to spend evangelizing

**Rule:** for indie SaaS at <$10K MRR, competing in an existing category with narrow positioning is almost always the right move. Category design is a luxury for funded teams.

## The strategic narrative: the structure that works

Andy Raskin's framework for the "only narrative that sells":

1. **Name a BIG, relevant change in the world.** Not about you — about the world. "Shopify now has 2M shops. Video content is now the #1 conversion driver. Most shops still can't make video because production is expensive."
2. **Show there will be winners and losers.** "The shops that adapt to video will capture 2x more conversions. The shops that don't will lose share to competitors who do."
3. **Tease the promised land.** "Imagine if every Shopify shop could ship a professional product video in 60 seconds for under $1." This is the aspirational state — not the product yet.
4. **Introduce the obstacles to the promised land.** "Today, video production takes 4+ hours per product. It requires editing skills. It requires studios. It requires models." These are the REAL obstacles, not fake ones.
5. **Present evidence you can lead them to the promised land.** Your product + customer stories + the path. THIS is where the product shows up — not at step 1.

The mistake most founders make: they start at step 5. They lead with the product. The problem is that step 5 only makes sense AFTER steps 1-4. If you haven't established the stakes, the product has no meaning.

**Write a strategic narrative once, use it in every pitch, every landing page, every blog post, every investor meeting.** It's the spine of your entire marketing.

## Category design traps

Things that look like category design but aren't:

1. **Renaming an existing category.** "Conversational marketing" (Drift) was a new category because it introduced a new interaction model. "AI-powered marketing automation" is NOT a new category — it's a feature of the existing one. Adding "AI" or "smart" or "next-gen" to an old category name is marketing theater.

2. **Inventing a category for a feature.** Your product is not a category. Your feature is definitely not a category. A category requires a NEW WAY OF SOLVING A PROBLEM, not a new button.

3. **Category design without a POV document.** If you can't write 2-3 pages on "why the old way is broken," you don't have a category — you have a product idea.

4. **Category design without early adopter co-signers.** If no one else will say "the old way is broken," you're alone with your manifesto, which is indistinguishable from being wrong.

5. **Expecting analysts to name your category for you.** Gartner doesn't create categories — they validate them after enough companies pay for research coverage. The vendor creates the category, then analysts ratify it.

## The minimal category design test (for indie SaaS)

If you want to attempt light-touch category design as a solo founder:

1. Write a 1-page POV document: "Today, [audience] does [old thing]. This is broken because [3 reasons]. The new way is [2-sentence description]. This is called [category name]."
2. Show it to 10 people in your ICP. Do their eyes light up? Do they ask "who else is saying this?" Or do they say "oh yeah, that's called [existing category name]"?
3. If they map it back to an existing category, you're not in new category territory — you're in positioning territory. Stop, go read `01-positioning.md`, and compete in the existing category with narrow positioning.
4. If they light up, that's a weak signal. Write a blog post. See if it gets shared organically with language that echoes your POV. That's a stronger signal.
5. Only commit to full category design if you have consistent "yes, that's what I've been saying but couldn't name" reactions from 5+ ICP interviews.

Most indie founders will fail this test, and that's FINE. Category design is hard for funded companies with full marketing teams. Failing the test means compete on positioning instead, which is just as viable and far cheaper.

## The honest summary

- **Category design is real and produces massive winners** (Salesforce, HubSpot, Drift, Gong, Slack-via-"work-messaging," Figma-via-"multiplayer-design").
- **For every winner, 50 companies failed trying.** You don't hear about them.
- **It's a funded-company move, not a bootstrapped-solo-founder move.** The cost of education is too high for someone with 3 hours a day.
- **Narrow positioning in an existing category is the bootstrapper's version of category design.** It gives you many of the benefits (specificity, owning a niche) without the cost of market education.
- **Read this file mostly to understand what category kings are doing when they dominate your space**, not to imitate them. If you compete against a category king in their category, you lose. Position around them, not through them.
