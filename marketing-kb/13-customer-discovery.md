# 13 — Customer Discovery

**When to use:** Before building a feature nobody asked for. Before writing copy. Before running ads. When your product isn't selling and you don't know why. When your retention is bad and you don't know why. When you're about to pivot. Customer discovery is the single highest-leverage activity for a pre-PMF product and almost every indie founder does it badly or not at all.

**Primary sources:** Rob Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test* (2013) — the book every founder should read before their first customer interview. Clayton Christensen's *Competing Against Luck* (Jobs-to-be-Done). Tony Ulwick's *Outcome-Driven Innovation* (the data-oriented JTBD school). Teresa Torres's *Continuous Discovery Habits* (modern interview discipline).

## Why most customer interviews are worthless

When a founder asks their mom "would you use this?", mom says yes. Your friends say yes. Your Twitter followers say yes. Strangers on a Zoom call say yes. And then nobody buys.

The problem: **people lie in customer interviews, but they don't lie about their lives.** They'll happily say "yes that sounds great" to be polite, to feel helpful, to end the call. They won't lie about what they already spent money on last month, what they complained about on Slack last week, or what they googled at 2 AM.

The Mom Test is a simple rule: **if a question could get a meaningful answer from your mom — even though your mom has no idea what your startup does — the question is a good question. If your mom would say "oh that sounds nice dear," the question is useless.**

## The three Mom Test rules

From Rob Fitzpatrick's book, these are the only rules that matter:

1. **Talk about their life, not your idea.** The moment you describe your idea, the conversation becomes about the idea (which they know nothing about) instead of their life (which they know everything about). Don't describe what you're building. Ask about their current workflow, their recent problems, their recent purchases.

2. **Ask about specifics in the past, not generics or opinions about the future.** "What would you pay for a tool that..." → worthless. "When did you last run into this problem?" → good. "How did you handle it?" → great. "What did you try first?" → excellent. The past is data; the future is speculation.

3. **Talk less, listen more.** Your job is to shut up and extract evidence, not to sell your idea. If you're talking more than 30% of the time, you're failing. Ask open-ended questions, then wait for them to fill the silence. Most of the best data comes in the second half of their answer, after the polite part is done.

## Bad questions vs good questions

**BAD:** "Do you think it's a good idea?"
**GOOD:** "How do you currently solve this?"

**BAD:** "Would you pay for a product that does X?"
**GOOD:** "What have you already spent money on to solve this?"

**BAD:** "How often does this problem happen?"
**GOOD:** "When was the last time it happened? Tell me about that day."

**BAD:** "What features would you want?"
**GOOD:** "What does a perfect solution look like — and what do you do when you can't have it?"

**BAD:** "Would you use this?"
**GOOD:** "Who else on your team would need to approve this purchase?"

**BAD:** "Does this solve your problem?"
**GOOD:** "What would you have to stop doing to make time for this?"

Notice the pattern: bad questions ask for opinions about a hypothetical future. Good questions ask for facts about an observed past. Facts > opinions. Past > future.

## The anti-data: three things to ignore

Fitzpatrick calls these "compliments, generics, and fluff." They feel like signal but carry zero predictive value:

1. **Compliments.** "Wow, that's a great idea." "I love it." "This would be so useful." File these under "people being polite." They have no correlation with purchase intent. The moment you hear one, redirect: "Thanks — who else on your team has this problem?"

2. **Generics.** "I always do X." "I usually hate Y." "Everyone at my company needs Z." Humans generalize when they don't want to do the work of remembering specifics. Force specifics: "When did you last do that? Walk me through it."

3. **Fluff about the future.** "I would definitely use that." "I'd pay $50/mo for this." "I'd switch tomorrow." Anything about the future is fiction. The only useful question about the future is the one grounded in present commitment: "Can I come back in a week and show you a prototype? Could you introduce me to two colleagues who also have this problem?" Reluctance = unserious interest.

## Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD) — the underlying theory

Clayton Christensen's frame: **people don't buy products; they "hire" products to do a job**. Milkshakes aren't hired to be breakfast — they're hired to make a long commute less boring. Snickers bars aren't hired to be a snack — they're hired to be a bridge to dinner when you're starving.

For software, the job is almost never "the obvious feature." A project management tool might be hired to:
- Make the founder feel in control of chaos (emotional job)
- Give a team lead defensible metrics to report to their boss (social job)
- Replace a spreadsheet that broke when the team hit 12 people (functional job)

Three job dimensions to probe in every interview:
1. **Functional:** what task are they trying to complete?
2. **Emotional:** how do they want to feel during and after?
3. **Social:** how do they want to be seen by their boss, peers, customers?

If you only build for the functional job, your competitor who also serves the emotional and social jobs will win. Every time.

## The Switch interview — the highest-signal interview type

The single most useful interview question for a product that replaces an existing solution: **"Tell me about the day you decided you had to switch from [old tool]."**

This works because switching is painful. People don't switch unless there's a *forcing event*. The forcing event contains 100% of the relevant information: what broke, how they noticed, how long they tolerated it, what the last straw was, who else was involved, what they tried first, what they almost bought instead, and what finally made them pull the trigger.

Every data point you need to sell to the next customer is in someone's switch story. Collect 10-15 of them before writing any copy.

## How to get interviews as an indie with no network

The hardest part is getting on the call. Cold methods that actually work:

1. **LinkedIn DMs with a specific ask.** "Hi [Name], I'm building something for [specific segment] and I saw your post about [specific pain]. Could I buy you a coffee/send you an Amazon gift card for 20 minutes of your time? I'm not selling anything — I just want to understand how you currently handle [X]." Reply rate: 5-15%. Gift card makes it 2x higher.

2. **Reddit / IndieHackers / niche forum DMs.** Find someone who posted a problem relevant to your product. DM: "I saw your post about X. I'm researching this space — would you be open to a 15-minute call to tell me more?" Reply rate: 10-25% in small communities.

3. **Your existing customers' colleagues.** If you have even one customer, ask for introductions. "Do you know two other people with this problem? I'd love to learn from them." Warm intros convert 4-5x better than cold.

4. **Communities where your users hang out.** Join the Slack groups, Discord servers, Circle communities where your target user already is. Don't pitch. Participate. Answer questions. After 2-3 weeks, DM people you've already helped: "I've been working on something related to [topic]. Would you be willing to chat for 15 minutes?"

5. **Offer to watch them work.** This is the nuclear option. "Can I sit in on a 30-minute session where you do [task] so I can see how it actually works today?" Agreement rate: 20% but the data is 10x richer than any verbal answer.

## What to do with interview notes

Raw notes from 10-15 interviews are useless unless you synthesize them. The discipline:

1. **Write up each interview within 24 hours** while memory is fresh. One markdown file per interview.
2. **Extract quotes verbatim.** Real customer quotes are gold for copy and for debating feature decisions internally.
3. **Tag each insight by category:** forcing event, current workaround, emotional job, deal-killer, pricing signal, competitor mentioned.
4. **Look for patterns across 5+ interviews.** A single interview is a data point; a pattern is a signal.
5. **Update your positioning statement** based on the patterns. If the interviews don't match your positioning, the positioning is wrong — not the interviews.

The goal: a 2-page synthesis document you can hand to yourself in 6 months and remember who you were building for. Most founders skip this step and then can't remember why they built what they built.

## The 5-interview rule

You don't need 100 interviews. You need 5 high-quality switch-interviews with your ideal customer profile. If 5 interviews give you 5 wildly different stories, you don't have product-market fit — you're still searching. If 5 interviews give you 5 stories with the same forcing event, same workaround, same frustration, and same decision criteria, you've found a segment and you should stop interviewing and start shipping for them.

**Rule:** 5 customer interviews will teach you more than 500 hours of staring at your analytics dashboard. Do them BEFORE you optimize your landing page, not after.

## When customer discovery is NOT useful

Customer discovery is for validating the problem and the buyer. It is NOT useful for:
- Deciding what color a button should be (A/B test instead)
- Validating visual design (usability test instead)
- Finding product-market fit (that's a retention cohort analysis, not an interview)
- Predicting feature prioritization (that's a usage data job, not an interview)

Don't ask customers what features to build. Ask them what problems they have. You pick the features.
